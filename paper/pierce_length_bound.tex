\title[The length of finite Pierce expansions]{Bounds for the Length of Finite Pierce Expansions}

\author{Jason Davies}
\email{jason@jasondavies.com}

\date{October 2026}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
For integers $a>b>0$, let $P(a,b)$ count the positive terms of the iteration
$x\mapsto a\bmod x$, starting at $b$. We prove
\[
 P(a,b)\ll_\varepsilon a^{\alpha_*+\varepsilon},\qquad
 \alpha_*=(1310-\sqrt{655})/4365=0.2942513246\ldots.
\]
Each tail of the orbit carries a finite positive measure whose Hankel
determinants are positive integers. We group short segments of the orbit by
small values of these determinants. Within a group, the division equations
$a=q_ib_i+b_{i+1}$, kept with the fixed dividend $a$, confine the quotient
increments to a union of boundedly many affine curves. Adjoining two further
integer determinants as coordinates raises the degree of these curves while
keeping the coordinates small. A curve-counting theorem whose constants do not
depend on the coefficients of the curve then bounds the number of segments
in each group. Raising the added coordinates to suitable powers and balancing
their sizes gives the exponent $\alpha_*$.
We also prove several elementary intermediate bounds, a bound for orbits
with few rises in their quotient increments, and positivity and integrality of
the Hankel determinants.
The main result and its curve-counting input are formalized in Lean~4 with
Mathlib without proof holes or additional axioms.
A separate refinement $P(a,b)\ll a^{3/10}\sqrt{\log\log 3a}$ is formalized
with two published analytic estimates as explicit hypotheses. Shallit's conjectured
$O((\log a)^2)$ bound remains open.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Let $a>b>0$ be integers. Put $b_0=b$ and $b_{i+1}=a \bmod b_i$ while $b_i>0$. The sequence is
strictly decreasing until it reaches $0$; if $b_n=0$ we write $P(a,b)=n$. For example
$P(35,22)=7$, from $22,13,9,8,3,2,1,0$. The number $P(a,b)$ is the length of the Pierce
expansion of $b/a$~\cite{Pierce,Shallit86}, since
\[
\frac ba=\frac1{q_0}-\frac1{q_0q_1}+\frac1{q_0q_1q_2}-\cdots,\qquad q_i=\lfloor a/b_i\rfloor .
\]
Shallit~\cite{OPG} asked whether
\begin{equation}\label{eq:conj}
P(a,b)=O\bigl((\log a)^2\bigr).
\end{equation}

Shallit~\cite{Shallit86} proved $P(a,b)\ll a^{1/2}$ by ``Archimedean'' arguments.
Erd\H{o}s and Shallit~\cite{ES} combined these with a divisor argument to get
$P(a,b)\ll_\varepsilon a^{1/3+\varepsilon}$, and showed that $\max_b P(a,b)/\log a$ does not
tend to $0$. Chase and Pandey~\cite{CP} went past the exponent $1/3$, proving
$P(a,b)\ll_\varepsilon a^{1/3-2/177+\varepsilon}$ with exponential sum estimates
(Bourgain's exponent pair), and showed that $\max_b P(a,b)\gg \log a/\log\log a$ for all large
$a$. Baraskar and Vukusic~\cite{BV} study the related sets of iterated remainders and
bound their cardinalities at each fixed iteration depth.
Shallit's talk pages~\cite{ShallitTalks} report a bound $n^{3/10}$, described as
``LLM, unpublished so far''. We have not seen that argument. The preliminary $3/10$ theorem below has the same
exponent up to $\varepsilon$, and we make no claim about how the two arguments are related.

Our strongest bound comes from the joint-invariant curve embedding of
Section~\ref{sec:joint}.
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:joint}
For every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $C_\varepsilon>0$, independent of $a,b$, such that
\[
 P(a,b)\le C_\varepsilon a^{(1310-\sqrt{655})/4365+\varepsilon}
 \qquad(0<b<a).
\]
\end{theorem}

Here $(1310-\sqrt{655})/4365=0.2942513\ldots$. The proof produces, for each
pair of positive integers $(m,n)$, an explicit rational exponent
(Corollary~\ref{cor:jointexponent}); the pairs $(1,1)$ and $(50,51)$ give
$128/435$ and $2201/7480$, which are slightly larger than $\alpha_*$. The
formalisation states these two instances separately, alongside
Theorem~\ref{thm:joint}.

\subsection*{The joint-invariant argument}
We count orbit terms in a remainder window $[A,2A)$ and then sum over dyadic
values of $A$; let $T$ be the number of terms in the window. For most
indices $i$ in the window, the next few quotient increments
$k_j=q_{i+j+1}-q_{i+j}$ are at most $H\asymp a/(AT)$. Each tail of the orbit
carries a positive measure (Section~\ref{sec:hankel}) whose Hankel
determinants $I_r(i)$ are positive integers; $I_1$, $I_2$ and $I_3$ are the
remainder, the drop, and the curvature $D$ of three consecutive steps
(Section~\ref{sec:curv}). The ten $3\times3$ minors of an explicit
$3\times5$ integer matrix of drops and increments have size at most
$X\asymp A^4H^6/a^3$ (Section~\ref{sec:827}). Their vector is the
\emph{pattern} of $i$, and there are $a^{o(1)}X^5$ possible patterns.

Fix a pattern. The increments are then determined by the three
\emph{nodes} $s_j=q_{i+j}-q_i$, $j=1,2,3$, which are integers in $[0,3H]$.
The nodes satisfy a quadratic equation coming from linear relations among
the minors. Keeping the fixed dividend $a$, they also satisfy the
degree-twelve equation $N_0\operatorname{Res}(P_4,xR_4)=a\Psi^4$, where
$N_0=I_4(i)$, $P_4$ and $R_4$ are the degree-four orthogonal polynomials of two
consecutive tails, and $\Psi$ is the squared norm of $P_4$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:jointcurve}). So the nodes lie on at most $24$ curves, each of
degree at most $24$. Counting integer points on these curves in the box
$[0,3H]^3$ directly saves at most a factor $H^{1/2}$ per curve, because a
component may have degree two.

To do better, we adjoin further integer coordinates: three $2\times2$
minors $W=(W_0,W_1,W_2)$ of drops and increments, one of which is the
curvature, of size at most $S\asymp A^3H^3/a^2$; and the fifth Hankel
determinant $J=I_5(i)$, of size at most $Y\asymp A^5H^{10}/a^4$. On a
component $C$ of degree $d$ these are rational functions of the nodes. At a
point of $C$ at infinity of multiplicity $e$, the $W_j$ vanish to order
$3e/2$ and $J$ has a pole of order $5e/2$. A linear combination of the $W_j$
also has one zero at an affine point, which forces one extra affine pole.
Consequently the curve obtained by adjoining $W_0^m,W_1^m,W_2^m,J^n$ to the
nodes has degree at least $10n+7m$, while its coordinates are at most
$\max(3H,S^m,Y^n)$. The bound of Castryck, Cluckers, Dittmann and
Nguyen~\cite{CCDN} counts the integer points of a curve of degree $\delta$ in
a box of side $B$ by $B^{1/\delta+o(1)}$, uniformly in the coefficients of the
curve. Applied to the enlarged curves, it bounds the number of starts per
pattern. Optimising over the ratio $n/m$ gives $\alpha_*$
(Section~\ref{sec:joint}).

The number of patterns, $a^{o(1)}X^5$, sets a floor for this method. Even as
$m,n\to\infty$, the pattern count alone gives $T\ll a^{5/17+o(1)}$, and
$\alpha_*$ exceeds $5/17=0.2941176\ldots$ by only $1.3\cdot10^{-4}$
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\subsection*{Elementary window bounds}
The simplest of our bounds is the following.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
For every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a constant $C_\varepsilon$ such that
\[
P(a,b)\le C_\varepsilon\, a^{3/10+\varepsilon}\qquad\text{for all integers }0<b<a .
\]
\end{theorem}

The proof is elementary. It uses only the division equations
$a=q_ib_i+b_{i+1}$ and the divisor bound $\tau(n)\ll_\delta n^\delta$. As usual, the remainders
are split into dyadic windows $[A,2A)$, and we bound the number $T$ of remainders in one
window. There are two bounds:
\begin{align}
T(T-1)&\le 4A\,\Delta, \label{eq:intro1}\\
T^6A^2&\ll a^3\Delta, \label{eq:intro2}
\end{align}
where $\Delta$ is the largest value of the divisor function on $[1,32a^7]$.
Bound~\eqref{eq:intro1} is the Erd\H{o}s--Shallit argument. Bound~\eqref{eq:intro2} is new.
Multiplying~\eqref{eq:intro2} by the square of~\eqref{eq:intro1} eliminates $A$ and gives
$T^{10}\ll a^3\Delta^3$, which is the exponent $3/10$.

The new bound comes from three consecutive steps $x=b_i$, $y=b_{i+1}$, $z=b_{i+2}$,
$w=b_{i+3}$. Let $d,e,f$ be the drops, let $u,v$ be the increments of the quotients, and put
$D=dv-eu$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intro-curv}
D\,z=df+ude+(u-1)e^2 .
\end{equation}
So $D$ is a \emph{positive} integer, even though $dv$ and $eu$ largely cancel, and it is small
when the drops are small. Also $(q,x)=(\lfloor a/b_i\rfloor,b_i)$ lies on the curve
$xM(q)=aN(q)+D$, where $M$ and $N$ are explicit polynomials depending on $(u,v)$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:fiber-eq}). A resultant-type identity shows that each such curve has
$a^{o(1)}$ admissible points (Lemma~\ref{lem:fiber}). The number of triples $(u,v,D)$ is
$\ll (B/T)^2\cdot a/T^3$, where $B=a/A$, which gives~\eqref{eq:intro2}.

\subsection*{A second positive integer}
Six consecutive terms carry three overlapping curvatures $D,E,F$ and four quotient increments
$u,v,w,z$. In Section~\ref{sec:six} we show that the integer
\[
L=\frac{S(v,w,z)\,D-S(u,v,w)\,E}{v},\qquad S(u,v,w)=u^2v+(u-1)v^2+uw,
\]
satisfies the exact identity $qwL=J(u,v,w,z)\,E+S(u,v,w)\,F$, where $J$ is a polynomial with
positive values (Lemma~\ref{lem:six}). Hence $L\ge1$, and $q\asymp a/A$ is bounded by a
polynomial in the small parameters $u,v,w,z,E,F$. A single window of six terms with small total
drop then gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intro3}
T^6A^2\ll a^3,
\end{equation}
with no divisor function at all (Proposition~\ref{prop:six}).

With~\eqref{eq:intro3} in hand, the only remaining loss is in the drop bound~\eqref{eq:intro1}. That
loss comes from the number of divisors of $a+h$ in $[A,2A)$ as $h$ ranges over a short interval.
Section~\ref{sec:short} shows that any bound $\ll y\,G(x)$ for such counts over intervals
$(x,x+y]$ with $y\ge x^{1/4}$, uniform in $A$ and with $G(x)\ll x^{1/40}$, gives
$P(a,b)\ll a^{3/10}G(a)^{1/5}$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:generic}).
The counts are majorised by the Erd\H{o}s--Hooley function. Combining the short-sum theorem of
Nair and Tenenbaum~\cite{NT} with the recent mean-value bound of de la Bret\`eche and
Tenenbaum~\cite{BT} gives $G(x)=(\log\log x)^{5/2}$, and hence the following.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:loglog}
Assume the theorem of Nair--Tenenbaum stated as~\eqref{eq:NT} and the theorem of de la
Bret\`eche--Tenenbaum stated as~\eqref{eq:BT} below. Then
\[
P(a,b)\ll a^{3/10}\sqrt{\log\log 3a}\qquad\text{uniformly for integers }0<b<a .
\]
\end{theorem}

Both hypotheses are published theorems. They are hypotheses only in the sense of the
formalisation: we have not formalised their proofs. Any other short-interval divisor bound
can be used in their place. For example, Shiu's theorem~\cite{Shiu} gives
$\sum_{x<n\le x+y}\tau(n)\ll y\log x$ for $y\ge x^{1/4}$, and hence
$P(a,b)\ll a^{3/10}(\log a)^{1/5}$; we have not formalised that either.

\subsection*{Structured trajectories and the Hankel hierarchy}
Write $k_i=q_{i+1}-q_i$ for the quotient increments, and put
\[
R=1+\#\{i:0\le i\le P(a,b)-3,\ k_i<k_{i+1}\}.
\]
Thus $R-1$ counts the rises between consecutive increments.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:rises}
$P(a,b)\ll a^{1/4}\sqrt R$ uniformly for $0<b<a$. In particular, if the quotient increments
never rise, then $P(a,b)\ll a^{1/4}$.
\end{theorem}

The proof (Section~\ref{sec:rises}) combines three facts. First, $D\ge1$ says that $d_i/k_i$ is
strictly decreasing, so the drops strictly decrease along every stretch without a rise. Second,
the six-step integer $L$ gives an exact identity for six quotients in arithmetic progression,
which forces their common difference to be large (Lemma~\ref{lem:ap}). Third, a sequence of
positive integers in which no value occurs too often has a large sum (Lemma~\ref{lem:mult}).
Conversely, Theorem~\ref{thm:rises} shows that an orbit of length about $a^{3/10}$ must
contain $\gg a^{1/10}$ rises.

Finally, both $D$ and $L$ fit into a single structure (Section~\ref{sec:hankel}). The orbit
carries an exact finite model by positive measures $\nu_i$ with $\int d\nu_i=b_i/a$. The Hankel
determinants $I_r(i)=a\det\bigl(\int x^{j+l}\,d\nu_i\bigr)_{j,l<r}$ are positive
integers whenever the remaining orbit has at least $r$ positive terms.
The first four agree with $b_i$, $b_i-b_{i+1}$, $D$ and $L$ in the
nonterminal ranges specified in Theorem~\ref{thm:hankel}.

\subsection*{Below \texorpdfstring{$3/10$}{3/10}}
The following four bounds lie between Theorems~\ref{thm:main} and~\ref{thm:joint}.
They introduce, one at a time, the pattern-counting ingredients that
Theorem~\ref{thm:joint} builds on, together with a lattice refinement that it does not need.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:2377}
For every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a constant $C_\varepsilon$ such that
\[
P(a,b)\le C_\varepsilon\, a^{23/77+\varepsilon}\qquad\text{for all integers }0<b<a .
\]
\end{theorem}

Here $23/77=0.298701\ldots$, a saving of $1/770$ over $3/10$. The proof (Section~\ref{sec:2377})
uses the fourth Hankel invariant $I_4=L$ as an integer, not only as a positive number. At the
critical configuration $L$ has bounded size. The labels $L(i),L(i+1),L(i+2)$ and four
cofactors of an explicit $3\times4$ integer matrix form a \emph{pattern} of seven integers. Two
consecutive states give two linear equations and one quadratic equation in five consecutive
quotient increments, and the quadratic has a positive leading coefficient. An exact identity
$I_4(i)\,\pi_i(-q_i)=a\det(\mu_i(x^{j+l}))_{j,l<4}$, with $\pi_i$ the degree-four orthogonal
polynomial of the moment model, makes $q_i$ a root of a quartic that is fixed by the pattern and
the increments. So each pattern accounts for $O(H^2)$ states, where $H$ bounds the increments.
The number of patterns is controlled by Hadamard's inequality applied to the moment model.

Counting the solutions of the quadratic equation more carefully gives a further improvement.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:1137}
For every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a constant $C_\varepsilon$ such that
\[
P(a,b)\le C_\varepsilon\, a^{11/37+\varepsilon}\qquad\text{for all integers }0<b<a .
\]
\end{theorem}

Here $11/37=0.297297\ldots$, a saving of $1/370$ over $3/10$. The extra input
(Section~\ref{sec:1137}) is that the quadratic part is nondegenerate with an indefinite binary restriction,
by exact sign identities for the cofactors. So each slice is a binary norm equation, which has
$a^{o(1)}$ solutions of size $a^{O(1)}$, uniformly in its coefficients, when its right-hand
side is nonzero. There are at most two exceptional slices, which are counted separately.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:827}
For every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a constant $C_\varepsilon$ such that
\[
P(a,b)\le C_\varepsilon\, a^{8/27+\varepsilon}\qquad\text{for all integers }0<b<a .
\]
\end{theorem}

Here $8/27=0.296296\ldots$. The seven pattern integers are signed coordinates of the vector of
$3\times3$ minors of a $3\times5$ integer matrix (Section~\ref{sec:827}). All ten minors are
bounded by $X$, and such minor vectors number $X^{5+o(1)}$ rather than $X^7$. From
Section~\ref{sec:827} on, the \emph{pattern} of a state means this vector of ten minors, which
determines the seven integers above.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:45152}
For every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a constant $C_\varepsilon$ such that
\[
P(a,b)\le C_\varepsilon\, a^{45/152+\varepsilon}\qquad\text{for all integers }0<b<a .
\]
\end{theorem}

Here $45/152=0.296052\ldots$. The pivot gaps of a state lie in a lattice $\Lambda_p\subseteq\mathbb
Z^3$ of index at most $X$ determined by its minor vector $p$ (Section~\ref{sec:45152}). If the
shortest vector of $\Lambda_p$ has length $\lambda$, a reduced basis cuts the ternary quadratic
into $O(H/\lambda+1)$ slices, and only $X^{4+o(1)}t^3$ minor vectors have $\lambda\le t$. This
gives $T\ll a^{o(1)}(HX^{14/3}+X^5)$ in place of $T\ll a^{o(1)}HX^5$.

The joint-invariant argument returns to the ordinary minor-pattern count
of Section~\ref{sec:827} and improves the count within each pattern. Its
additional input is the affine-curve theorem used in Section~\ref{sec:joint}.

\section{Preliminaries}

\subsection{Notation}
Throughout, $a>b>0$ are integers, $b_0=b$, $b_{i+1}=a\bmod b_i$, and $P=P(a,b)$, so that
$a>b_0>b_1>\dots>b_{P-1}>0=b_P$. Write $q_i=\lfloor a/b_i\rfloor$, so
\[
a=q_ib_i+b_{i+1},\qquad 0\le b_{i+1}<b_i\qquad(0\le i<P).
\]
An index $i$ of the orbit is also called a \emph{state}; quantities ``at state $i$'' are
computed from $b_i,b_{i+1},\dots$ and $q_i,q_{i+1},\dots$, and are defined whenever the
orbit terms they use exist.
Let $\tau(n)$ be the number of positive divisors of $n$, and let
\[
\Delta=\Delta(a)=\max_{1\le n\le 32a^7}\tau(n).
\]
We write $F\ll_\eta G$ when $F\le C_\eta G$, with the constant uniform in the
dividend and orbit. A factor denoted $a^{o(1)}$ is bounded by
$C_\eta a^\eta$ for every fixed $\eta>0$; such factors include the divisor
losses below. We write $F\asymp G$ when both $F\ll G$ and $G\ll F$ hold.
Many statements below carry explicit numerical constants; these are the constants of the
Lean proof. Where a constant is large we state the bound with $\ll$ and record the constant
in the proof.

\begin{lemma}[Quotients increase]\label{lem:quot}
If $b_{i+1}>0$ then $q_{i+1}\ge q_i+1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $b_{i+1}<b_i$ we have $(q_i+1)b_{i+1}\le q_ib_i+b_{i+1}=a$, so
$q_{i+1}=\lfloor a/b_{i+1}\rfloor\ge q_i+1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Divisor bound]\label{lem:div}
For every integer $k\ge1$ there is $C_k$ with $\tau(n)^k\le C_k\,n$ for all $n\ge1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
This is standard: $\tau(n)^k/n=\prod_{p^\alpha\| n}(\alpha+1)^k/p^\alpha$. Each factor with
$p\ge 2^k$ is at most $(\alpha+1)^k/2^{k\alpha}\le1$. Each of the fewer than $2^k$ factors with
$p<2^k$ is at most $\sup_\alpha(\alpha+1)^k/2^\alpha<\infty$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Window runs}

\begin{definition}
Let $A\ge1$ be an integer. A \emph{window run} of length $T$ is a sequence $x_0,x_1,\dots,x_{T-1}$ of
consecutive terms of the sequence $(b_i)$, so $x_{j+1}=a\bmod x_j$, all lying in $[A,2A)$.
\end{definition}

In a window run, $x_0>x_1>\dots>x_{T-1}$ and $x_j\le a$.

\section{The curvature of three consecutive steps}\label{sec:curv}

Fix four consecutive terms
\[
x=b_i,\quad y=b_{i+1}=a\bmod x,\quad z=b_{i+2}=a\bmod y,\quad w=b_{i+3}=a\bmod z,
\]
with $x,y,z>0$ (and $w\ge0$). Write
\[
q=\left\lfloor \frac ax\right\rfloor,\quad
u=\left\lfloor \frac ay\right\rfloor-q,\quad
v=\left\lfloor \frac az\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor \frac ay\right\rfloor,\qquad
d=x-y,\quad e=y-z,\quad f=z-w .
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:quot}, $u,v\ge1$, and clearly $d,e,f\ge1$. The division equations read
\begin{equation}\label{eq:div}
xq+y=a,\qquad y(q+u)+z=a,\qquad z(q+u+v)+w=a .
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}
The \emph{curvature} of the block is $D=dv-eu$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Curvature identity]\label{lem:curv}
\[
D\,z=df+ude+(u-1)e^2 .
\]
In particular $D\ge1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Subtracting the first two equations of~\eqref{eq:div} gives $uy=qd+e$, hence
$uz=uy-ue=qd+(1-u)e$. Subtracting the last two gives $vz=(q+u)e+f$. Therefore
\[
(dv-eu)z=d\bigl((q+u)e+f\bigr)-e\bigl(qd+(1-u)e\bigr)=df+ude+(u-1)e^2 .
\]
The right-hand side is at least $df\ge1$, and $z>0$, so $D>0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The fiber equation]\label{lem:fiber-eq}
Put $M(q)=uq^2+(u^2+u+v)q+v$ and $N(q)=uq+u^2+v$. Then
\[
x\,M(q)=a\,N(q)+D .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
From~\eqref{eq:div}, $d=(q+1)x-a$ and $e=y-z=(q+u+1)y-a=a(q+u)-q(q+u+1)x$. Substituting
these into $D=dv-eu$ gives
$D=x\bigl(uq(q+u+1)+v(q+1)\bigr)-a\bigl(u(q+u)+v\bigr)$, which is the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Size of the parameters]\label{lem:size}
If $x\le a$ then
\[
u\,yx\le a(d+e),\qquad v\,zy\le a(e+f),\qquad D\,z\le (u+1)(d+e+f)^2 .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
From $uy=qd+e$ we get $uyx=(qx)d+ex\le ad+ae$, since $qx\le a$ and $x\le a$. Similarly
$vz=(q+u)e+f$ with $(q+u)y\le a$ and $y\le a$ gives the second bound. For the third, by
Lemma~\ref{lem:curv},
$Dz=df+ude+(u-1)e^2\le df+u\,e(d+e)\le (1+u)(d+e+f)^2$.
\end{proof}

\section{Few blocks share a triple \texorpdfstring{$(u,v,D)$}{(u,v,D)}}

\begin{lemma}[Fiber count]\label{lem:fiber}
Let $a\ge1$, $1\le u,v\le a$ and $1\le D\le 2a$. Let $M(q)=uq^2+pq+v$ with $p=u^2+u+v$, and
let $\Lambda(q)=cq+\ell$ with $c=au$ and $\ell=a(u^2+v)+D$. Then the set
\[
Q=\{q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}:\ xM(q)=\Lambda(q)\text{ for some integer }x\ge1\}
\]
has at most $\Delta=\max_{1\le n\le 32a^7}\tau(n)$ elements.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $U(q)=ucq+cp-u\ell$ and $R=u\ell^2-cp\ell+c^2v$. Expanding shows
\begin{equation}\label{eq:resultant}
c^2M(q)-U(q)\Lambda(q)=R
\end{equation}
identically in $q$. If $q\in Q$ with witness $x$, substituting $\Lambda(q)=xM(q)$
into~\eqref{eq:resultant} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:R}
R=M(q)\bigl(c^2-U(q)x\bigr).
\end{equation}
We bound the sizes first. From $u,v\le a$ and $D\le 2a$ we get $\ell\le 4a^3$, $p\le 3a^2$
and $c\le a^2$, so
\[
|R|\le u\ell^2+cp\ell+c^2v\le 16a^7+12a^7+a^5\le 32a^7,\qquad c^2\le a^4\le 32a^7 .
\]

\emph{Case $R\ne0$.} By~\eqref{eq:R}, $M(q)$ is a positive divisor of $|R|$. Since $u,p\ge1$,
the map $q\mapsto M(q)$ is strictly increasing on $q\ge0$, hence injective. Therefore
$|Q|\le\tau(|R|)\le\Delta$.

\emph{Case $R=0$.} Since $M(q)>0$, \eqref{eq:R} gives $U(q)x=c^2$. Here $c^2>0$ and $x\ge1$,
so $U(q)$ is a positive divisor of $c^2$. Since $U$ is linear with positive leading
coefficient $uc$, it is injective. Therefore $|Q|\le\tau(c^2)\le\Delta$.
\end{proof}

The case $R=0$ cannot be dropped: it is the case in which $M$ and $\Lambda$ might share a factor.

\section{Counting visits to a window}

In this section $x_0,\dots,x_{T-1}$ is a window run in $[A,2A)$, and
$q_j=\lfloor a/x_j\rfloor$ are its quotients. We continue to write
$\Delta=\max_{1\le n\le 32a^7}\tau(n)$.

\begin{proposition}[Drop bound; after Erd\H{o}s--Shallit]\label{prop:drop}
If $T\ge2$ then $T(T-1)\le 4A\Delta$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $h_j=x_j-x_{j+1}\ge1$ for $0\le j\le T-2$. By telescoping,
$\sum_j h_j=x_0-x_{T-1}\le (2A-1)-A=A-1$. Call $j$ \emph{good} if $h_j(T-1)\le 2A$ and
\emph{bad} otherwise. Each bad $j$ has $h_j(T-1)\ge 2A+1$, so
\[
|\Bad|\,(2A+1)\le (T-1)\textstyle\sum_j h_j\le (T-1)(A-1).
\]
If $2|\Bad|\ge T-1$ this would give $(T-1)(2A+1)\le 2(T-1)(A-1)$, which is false. Hence
$2|\Bad|<T-1$, and since $|\Good|+|\Bad|=T-1$ we get $2|\Good|\ge T$.

For good $j$ we have $1\le h_j\le H:=\lfloor 2A/(T-1)\rfloor$. Also
$a+h_j=q_jx_j+x_{j+1}+x_j-x_{j+1}=(q_j+1)x_j$, so $x_j\mid a+h_j$. Moreover
$a+h_j\le 2a\le 32a^7$. Since $j\mapsto x_j$ is injective, at most $\tau(a+h)\le\Delta$ good
indices have $h_j=h$. Hence $|\Good|\le H\Delta$, and
\[
T(T-1)\le 2|\Good|(T-1)\le 2\Delta\,H(T-1)\le 4A\Delta. \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Curvature bound]\label{prop:curv}
If $T\ge12$ then $T^6A^2\le 995328\,a^3\Delta$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $0\le j\le T-4$ let $s_j=x_j-x_{j+3}$ be the total drop of the block
$x_j,x_{j+1},x_{j+2},x_{j+3}$. By telescoping,
\[
\sum_{j=0}^{T-4}s_j=(x_0+x_1+x_2)-(x_{T-3}+x_{T-2}+x_{T-1})\le 3(2A-1)-3A=3A-3 .
\]
Call $j$ \emph{good} if $s_jT\le 12A$ and \emph{bad} otherwise. Then
$|\Bad|(12A+1)\le T(3A-3)$. If $4|\Bad|\ge T$ this would give $T(12A+1)\le 4T(3A-3)$, which is
false. So $4|\Bad|\le T-1$. Since $|\Good|=T-3-|\Bad|$ and $T\ge12$,
\[
2|\Good|\ge 2T-6-\tfrac{T-1}2\ge T .
\]

Fix a good $j$. Apply Section~\ref{sec:curv} to $x=x_j$, so that $y,z,w=x_{j+1},x_{j+2},x_{j+3}$, all
in $[A,2A)$. Write $s=s_j=d+e+f$. Lemma~\ref{lem:size} gives $uA^2\le uyx\le a(d+e)\le as$.
Multiplying by $T$ and using $sT\le 12A$ gives $uA^2T\le 12aA$, that is,
\[
u\,AT\le 12a,\qquad\text{and likewise}\qquad v\,AT\le 12a .
\]
Also $DA\le Dz\le(u+1)s^2\le 2us^2$. Multiplying by $T^2$ gives
$DAT^2\le 2u(sT)^2\le 288uA^2$, so $DT^2\le 288uA$, and then
\[
D\,T^3\le 288\,u\,AT\le 3456\,a .
\]
Thus the triple $(u,v,D)$ of a good block lies in $[1,U]^2\times[1,D^*]$, where
\[
U=\lfloor 12a/(AT)\rfloor\le a,\qquad D^*=\lfloor 3456a/T^3\rfloor\le 2a .
\]
The inequalities $U\le a$ and $D^*\le 2a$ hold because $AT\ge12$ and $T^3\ge1728$.

Now fix such a triple and consider the good blocks that have it. By Lemma~\ref{lem:fiber-eq},
each one gives a point $q=q_j=\lfloor a/x_j\rfloor$ of the set $Q$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:fiber}.
The map $j\mapsto q_j$ is injective on these blocks: $q_j$ determines $x_j=\Lambda(q_j)/M(q_j)$, and
$x_j$ determines $j$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:fiber}, at most $\Delta$ good blocks share a triple.
So $|\Good|\le U^2D^*\Delta$, and
\[
T^6A^2=T\,(AT)^2\,T^3\le 2\Delta\,(U\,AT)^2\,(D^*T^3)\le 2\Delta(12a)^2(3456a)=995328\,a^3\Delta.
\qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Window bound]\label{cor:window}
If $T\ge12$ then $T^{10}\le 63700992\,a^3\Delta^3$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Using $T\le 2(T-1)$ and the two propositions,
\[
T^{10}A^2\le 4\,(T^6A^2)\,\bigl(T(T-1)\bigr)^2\le 4\cdot 995328\,a^3\Delta\cdot(4A\Delta)^2
=63700992\,a^3\Delta^3A^2 .
\]
Divide by $A^2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
In terms of $B=a/A$, Propositions~\ref{prop:drop} and~\ref{prop:curv} say
$T\ll a^{1/2+o(1)}B^{-1/2}$ and $T\ll a^{1/6+o(1)}B^{1/3}$. The first is better when
$B>a^{2/5}$, and both equal $a^{3/10+o(1)}$ at $B=a^{2/5}$. Corollary~\ref{cor:window} takes
the product of the two bounds, so no case split is needed.
\end{remark}

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}}

Let $0<b<a$. For $0\le k\le\log_2 a$ let $T_k$ be the number of indices $i<P$ with
$2^k\le b_i<2^{k+1}$. Every $b_i$ with $i<P$ lies in $[1,a)$, so
$P=\sum_{0\le k\le \log_2 a}T_k$. Since $(b_i)$ is decreasing, the indices counted by $T_k$
are consecutive. So when $T_k>0$ they form a window run in $[A,2A)$ with $A=2^k$.

Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose an integer $m>7/(5\varepsilon)$, and let $C_{3m}$ be the constant of
Lemma~\ref{lem:div} with $k=3m$. The maximum $\Delta$ is attained at some $n_0\le 32a^7$, so
$\Delta^{3m}\le C_{3m}\cdot 32a^7$. If $T_k\ge12$, Corollary~\ref{cor:window} gives
\[
T_k^{10m}\le 63700992^m\,a^{3m}\,\Delta^{3m}\le K\,a^{3m+7},\qquad K=63700992^m\cdot C_{3m}\cdot 32,
\]
and hence
\[
T_k\le K^{1/(10m)}\,a^{3/10+7/(10m)}\le K^{1/(10m)}\,a^{3/10+\varepsilon/2}.
\]
If $T_k\le11$ this bound holds with $11$ in place of $K^{1/(10m)}$. Finally
$\lfloor\log_2a\rfloor+1\le C'_\varepsilon a^{\varepsilon/2}$. Summing over $k$,
\[
P(a,b)\le (\lfloor\log_2 a\rfloor+1)\bigl(11+K^{1/(10m)}\bigr)a^{3/10+\varepsilon/2}
\le C_\varepsilon\, a^{3/10+\varepsilon}. \qquad\qed
\]

\section{Six consecutive steps}\label{sec:six}

Fix six consecutive terms $x_0>x_1>\dots>x_5\ge0$ of the sequence $(b_i)$ with $x_4>0$, and
write their quotients as
\begin{gather*}
\lfloor a/x_0\rfloor=q,\quad \lfloor a/x_1\rfloor=q+u,\quad \lfloor a/x_2\rfloor=q+u+v,\\
\lfloor a/x_3\rfloor=q+u+v+w,\quad \lfloor a/x_4\rfloor=q+u+v+w+z .
\end{gather*}
(Unlike in Section~\ref{sec:curv}, here $w$ and $z$ denote quotient increments.) By
Lemma~\ref{lem:quot}, $u,v,w,z\ge1$. Let $f_k=x_k-x_{k+1}\ge1$ be the drops, and let
\[
D=f_0v-f_1u,\qquad E=f_1w-f_2v,\qquad F=f_2z-f_3w
\]
be the curvatures of the blocks starting at $x_0$, $x_1$ and $x_2$. Write $\mathcal E_k$ for the division
equation $x_kq_k+x_{k+1}-a=0$, where $q_k=\lfloor a/x_k\rfloor$. Put
\begin{align*}
S(u,v,w)&=u^2v+(u-1)v^2+uw,\\
J(u,v,w,z)&=\bigl(u^2+(u-1)v\bigr)z+w\bigl(u^3+uv(2u-1)+(u-1)v^2\bigr)+w^2\bigl((u-1)(u+v)+1\bigr),\\
L&=(vw+w^2+z)\,D-\bigl(u^2+(u-1)v\bigr)E-w\,(wf_0-uf_2).
\end{align*}

\begin{lemma}[The six-step identity]\label{lem:six}
\begin{enumerate}
\item $S(v,w,z)\,D-S(u,v,w)\,E=v\,L$.
\item $q\,w\,L=J(u,v,w,z)\,E+S(u,v,w)\,F$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Part~(1) is a polynomial identity: since $S(v,w,z)\equiv-w^2$ and $S(u,v,w)\equiv uw\pmod v$,
and $wD+uE=v(wf_0-uf_2)$, the difference is divisible by $v$, and expanding gives $L$. Part~(2)
holds modulo the division equations. Explicitly, $qwL-JE-S(u,v,w)F$ equals
\begin{align*}
&w(v^2w+vw^2+vz-w^2)\,\mathcal E_0-w(u^2w+2uvw+uw^2+uz+v^2w+vw^2-vw+vz-w^2)\,\mathcal E_1\\
&+w(u^2v+u^2w+uv^2+2uvw+uw^2+uw+uz-v^2-vw)\,\mathcal E_2-w(u^2v+uv^2+uw-v^2)\,\mathcal E_3 .
\end{align*}
This is an identity of polynomials in $a$, $q$, the $x_k$ and the increments, checked by expansion.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:six}
Suppose $q\ge1$ and $u,v,w,z\le m$. Then $E,F\ge1$, $L\ge1$, and
\[
q\le 9m^3E+3m^3F .
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Lemma~\ref{lem:curv}, applied to the blocks starting at $x_1$ and $x_2$, gives $E,F\ge1$.
Clearly $J>0$ and $S\ge0$, so by Lemma~\ref{lem:six}(2) $qwL>0$. Since $L$ is an integer,
$L\ge1$ and $qw\le JE+SF$. Bounding each monomial gives $J\le 9m^3w$ and $S(u,v,w)\le 3m^3w$.
Dividing by $w$ gives the claim.
\end{proof}

So the quotient $q$, which is about $a/A$, is controlled by the small parameters of a single block
of six terms. No counting over many blocks is needed.

\begin{proposition}[Six-step window bound]\label{prop:six}
For a window run of length $T\ge10$,
\[
T^6A^2\le 96\cdot10^6\,a^3 .
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $0\le j\le T-6$ let $s_j=x_j-x_{j+5}$. By telescoping,
$\sum_{j\le T-6}s_j\le 5(2A-1)-5A=5A-5$. So some $j$ has $s_j(T-5)\le 5A-5$, and since
$T\le 2(T-5)$, we get $s:=s_j$ with $sT\le 10A$. Apply Corollary~\ref{cor:six} to
$x_j,\dots,x_{j+5}$, all of which lie in $[A,2A)$, with $m=\max(u,v,w,z)$.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:size} at $x_j$, $x_{j+1}$ and $x_{j+2}$, each increment satisfies
$(\text{increment})\cdot A^2\le a s$. Multiplying by $T$ gives $mAT\le 10a$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:size} again, $EA\le Ex_{j+3}\le(v+1)s^2\le 2ms^2$, and likewise $FA\le 2ms^2$.
Hence $qA\le 24m^4s^2$. Also $a=qx_j+x_{j+1}<(q+1)x_j<2A(q+1)\le 4Aq$. Therefore
\[
qA\cdot A^4T^6\le 24\,(mAT)^4(sT)^2\le 24\cdot10^6\,a^4A^2,
\]
so $qA^3T^6\le 24\cdot 10^6a^4$, and $aA^2T^6\le 4qA^3T^6\le 96\cdot10^6\,a^4$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
In terms of $B=a/A$, Proposition~\ref{prop:six} reads $T\ll a^{1/6}B^{1/3}$. This is
Proposition~\ref{prop:curv} without the factor $\Delta^{1/6}$. Combined with
Proposition~\ref{prop:drop} as in Corollary~\ref{cor:window}, it gives $T^{10}\ll a^3\Delta^2$
and another proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. The Lean proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} uses
Corollary~\ref{cor:window}.
\end{remark}

\section{Divisors over short intervals}\label{sec:short}

For $A\ge1$ let $\tau_A(n)=\#\{d\mid n:\ A\le d<2A\}$.

\begin{proposition}[Drop count]\label{prop:dropcount}
For a window run of length $T\ge2$ and any $Y\ge\lfloor 2A/(T-1)\rfloor$,
\[
T\le 2\sum_{a<n\le a+Y}\tau_A(n).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
This is the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:drop}, stopped before the divisor bound. The good
indices $j$ have $1\le h_j\le\lfloor 2A/(T-1)\rfloor$, and $j\mapsto x_j$ maps those with $h_j=h$
injectively into the divisors of $a+h$ in $[A,2A)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
Let $G\colon\mathbb N\to\mathbb R$. We say that $\mathrm{SD}(G)$ holds if there are $C,x_0$ such
that for all integers $x\ge x_0$, $x^{1/4}\le y\le x$ and $A\ge1$,
\[
\sum_{x<n\le x+y}\tau_A(n)\le C\,y\,G(x).
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Geometric sums]\label{lem:geom}
Let $\alpha,\beta,s,t>0$ and $K\ge0$. Then
\[
\sum_{k<K}\min\bigl(\alpha2^{-sk},\ \beta2^{tk}\bigr)\le
\Bigl(\frac1{1-2^{-s}}+\frac{2^t}{2^t-1}\Bigr)\alpha^{t/(s+t)}\beta^{s/(s+t)} .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $M=\alpha^{t/(s+t)}\beta^{s/(s+t)}$. Then $(\alpha2^{-sk})^t(\beta2^{tk})^s=M^{s+t}$, so for each
$k$ either $\beta2^{tk}\le M$ or $\alpha2^{-sk}<M$. The terms $\beta2^{tk}\le M$ grow with ratio
$2^t$, so they sum to at most $M2^t/(2^t-1)$. The terms $\alpha2^{-sk}<M$ decay with ratio $2^{-s}$,
so they sum to at most $M/(1-2^{-s})$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:generic}
Suppose $\mathrm{SD}(G)$ holds, $G\ge1$, and $G(x)\ll x^{1/40}$. Then
$P(a,b)\ll a^{3/10}\,G(a)^{1/5}$ uniformly for $0<b<a$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Small $a$ are handled by $P(a,b)\le b<a$. Fix a dyadic window $[A,2A)$, $A=2^k$, with
$T\ge20$ visits. Proposition~\ref{prop:six} gives $T\le\alpha A^{-1/3}$, where
$\alpha=(96\cdot10^6)^{1/6}a^{1/2}$. Take $Y=\max(\lfloor 2A/(T-1)\rfloor,\lceil a^{1/4}\rceil)\le a$
in Proposition~\ref{prop:dropcount}. With $H=\lfloor 2A/(T-1)\rfloor$, $\mathrm{SD}(G)$ gives
$T\le 2CG(a)(H+a^{1/4}+1)$. Multiplying by $T-1$, using $H(T-1)\le2A$, and solving the quadratic
gives $T\le1+2CG(a)(a^{1/4}+1)+2\sqrt{CG(a)A}$, hence, as $T\ge20$, $T\le\gamma+\beta A^{1/2}$,
where $\beta=4\sqrt{CG(a)}$ and $\gamma=4CG(a)(a^{1/4}+1)$. So
every window satisfies
\[
T_k\le 19+\gamma+\min\bigl(\alpha\,2^{-k/3},\ \beta\,2^{k/2}\bigr).
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:geom} with $s=1/3$ and $t=1/2$, where $1/(1-2^{-1/3})+\sqrt2/(\sqrt2-1)<9$, gives
\[
\sum_{k}\min\bigl(\alpha\,2^{-k/3},\ \beta\,2^{k/2}\bigr)\le 9\,\alpha^{3/5}\beta^{2/5}\ll a^{3/10}G(a)^{1/5},
\]
with no factor $\log a$. The remaining terms contribute
$(\log_2a+1)(19+\gamma)\ll a^{1/100}\cdot a^{1/4}G(a)$. This is
$\ll a^{3/10}G(a)^{1/5}$, because $G(a)^{4/5}\ll a^{1/50}$.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{The Erd\H{o}s--Hooley function}
Let $\Delta_{\mathrm H}(n)=\max_{u\in\mathbb R}\#\{d\mid n:\ e^u<d\le e^{u+1}\}$. Taking
$e^{u+1}=2A$, and using $e>2$, gives $\tau_A(n)\le\Delta_{\mathrm H}(n)$. We use two published
theorems.

\begin{enumerate}
\item[(NT)] Nair and Tenenbaum~\cite{NT} prove a general upper bound for short sums of
functions $F$ satisfying $F(mn)\le\min(\lambda_1^{\Omega(m)},\lambda_2m^\varepsilon)F(n)$ for $(m,n)=1$. Since
$\Delta_{\mathrm H}(mn)\le\tau(m)\Delta_{\mathrm H}(n)$ for $(m,n)=1$, the function
$\Delta_{\mathrm H}$ satisfies this with $\lambda_1=2$ for every $\varepsilon>0$. Their bound, for the
polynomial $X$, gives for every fixed $\sigma>0$, $x$ large and $x^{\sigma}\le y\le x$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:NT}
\sum_{x<n\le x+y}\Delta_{\mathrm H}(n)\ll_\sigma\frac{y}{\log x}\sum_{n\le x}\frac{\Delta_{\mathrm H}(n)}{n}.
\end{equation}
We use it with $\sigma=1/4$. In the parameters of their Theorem~1, the
polynomial degree is $g=1$; take $\varepsilon=1/16$ and $\delta=1$.
Then $4g^2\varepsilon=1/4$ and the required
class is $\mathcal M_1(2,B,1/48)$ for a suitable $B$. The same consequence is displayed
immediately after equation~(2) of their paper.
\item[(BT)] de la Bret\`eche and Tenenbaum~\cite[Theorem~1.1]{BT} prove that for large $x$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BT}
\sum_{n\le x}\Delta_{\mathrm H}(n)\ll x\,(\log\log x)^{5/2}.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:partial}
Assume~\eqref{eq:BT}, and let $\ell(x)=\max(1,\log\log x)$. Then
$\sum_{n\le x}\Delta_{\mathrm H}(n)/n\ll\log x\cdot\ell(x)^{5/2}$ for large $x$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Split $n$ into dyadic blocks $2^j\le n<2^{j+1}\le 2x$. The terms with $n$ below the threshold
of~\eqref{eq:BT} contribute $O(1)$, since $\Delta_{\mathrm H}(n)\le n$. Each remaining block
contributes at most $2^{-j}\sum_{n\le 2^{j+1}}\Delta_{\mathrm H}(n)\ll\ell(2x)^{5/2}$, and
$\ell(2x)\le2\ell(x)$. There are at most $\log_2x+1\le 3\log x$ blocks.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:loglog}]
By~\eqref{eq:NT} and Lemma~\ref{lem:partial}, $\sum_{x<n\le x+y}\tau_A(n)\le
\sum_{x<n\le x+y}\Delta_{\mathrm H}(n)\ll y\,\ell(x)^{5/2}$ for $x^{1/4}\le y\le x$. So
$\mathrm{SD}(G)$ holds with $G=\ell^{5/2}$, which satisfies $G\ge1$ and $G(x)\ll x^{1/40}$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:generic} gives $P(a,b)\ll a^{3/10}\ell(a)^{1/2}\ll a^{3/10}\sqrt{\log\log3a}$.
\end{proof}

\section{Rises in the quotient increments}\label{sec:rises}

Throughout this section $k_i=q_{i+1}-q_i\ge1$, $d_i=b_i-b_{i+1}\ge1$, and a
\emph{rise} is an index $0\le i\le P(a,b)-3$ with $k_i<k_{i+1}$.

\begin{lemma}[Six quotients in arithmetic progression]\label{lem:ap}
Let $q,h\ge1$ and $x_0>x_1>\dots>x_6\ge0$ satisfy $x_j(q+jh)+x_{j+1}=a$ for $0\le j\le5$. Then
$q^4\le 324\,h^3a$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $f_j=x_j-x_{j+1}$, and let $E=(f_1-f_2)h$ and $F=(f_2-f_3)h$ be the curvatures of the blocks
starting at $x_1$ and $x_2$ (all their increments equal $h$). With $U=f_0-2f_1+f_2$, the
division equations $\mathcal E_j\colon x_j(q+jh)+x_{j+1}-a=0$ give the exact identity
\[
qhU=(3h-1)E+F,\qquad\text{namely}\qquad qhU-(3h-1)E-F=h(\mathcal E_0-3\mathcal E_1+3\mathcal E_2-\mathcal E_3).
\]
This is the case $u=v=w=z=h$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:six}, where $L=2h^3U$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:curv}, $E,F\ge1$, so the integer $U$ is at least $1$.

Now bound $E$ and $F$. Subtracting consecutive division equations gives
$q_jf_j\le hx_{j+1}$, where $q_j=q+jh$. Since $x_{j+1}q_{j+1}\le a$, this yields
$q^2f_j\le ha$ for $1\le j\le4$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:size},
$Ex_3\le(h+1)(f_1+f_2+f_3)^2\le 9h^2(h+1)a^2/q^4$. Also $a<x_3(q+3h+1)$, so
$Eq^4\le 9h^2(h+1)(q+3h+1)a$, and likewise $Fq^4\le 9h^2(h+1)(q+4h+1)a$. Hence
\[
q^5h\le q^4\bigl((3h-1)E+F\bigr)\le 27h^3(h+1)(q+4h+1)a .
\]
If $q\ge h$ this gives $q^4\le 324h^3a$. If $q<h$ then $q^4<h^4\le h^3a$, since $h\le q_1\le a$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:norise}
Whenever $b_{i+2}>0$, $d_{i+1}k_i<d_ik_{i+1}$. In particular, if $k_{i+1}\le k_i$ then
$d_{i+1}<d_i$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
This is $D=d_ik_{i+1}-d_{i+1}k_i\ge1$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:curv}). If $k_{i+1}\le k_i$ and
$d_{i+1}\ge d_i$, then $d_ik_{i+1}\le d_{i+1}k_i$, which is a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:mult}
Let $c_j\ge1$ be integers, $j\in J$, such that no value occurs more than $M$ times. Then
$|J|^2\le 2M\sum_{j\in J}c_j$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Induct on $|J|$. Let $c_{j_0}$ be the largest value. The values lie in $[1,c_{j_0}]$, so
$|J|\le Mc_{j_0}$. Removing $j_0$ and using $|J|^2=(|J|-1)^2+2|J|-1$ completes the induction.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:rises}
Let $x_0,\dots,x_{T-1}$ be a window run in $[A,2A)$ with $T\ge2$, and let $\rho$ be the number
of rises among its $T-1$ increments. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $(T-1)^2\le 2(\rho+1)(A-1)$;
\item $T-1\le\lfloor a/A\rfloor$;
\item if $6A\le a$, then $T-1=\ell+s$ with $\ell^2\le 8(\rho+1)\lfloor a/A\rfloor$ and
$s^3\le 26244\,A$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Split the increments into \emph{runs}: maximal stretches containing no rise. There are at most
$\rho+1$ runs.

(1) By Lemma~\ref{lem:norise}, the drops strictly decrease along a run, so each value of $d_j$
occurs at most once per run, hence at most $\rho+1$ times. Lemma~\ref{lem:mult} and
$\sum_jd_j=x_0-x_{T-1}\le A-1$ give the claim.

(2) The increments are $\ge1$, and they sum to $q_{T-1}-q_0\le\lfloor a/A\rfloor$.

(3) Call an increment $h$ \emph{low} if $26244h^3A^4<a^3$. Five consecutive equal increments
$h$ inside the run give six quotients in arithmetic progression. Lemma~\ref{lem:ap} then
gives $q^4\le324h^3a$. On the other hand, $a<3Aq$, which follows from $a=qx_0+x_1$,
$x_1<x_0<2A$ and $6A\le a$. Together these give $a^3\le26244h^3A^4$, so $h$ is not low.
Within a run the increments are nonincreasing, so equal values are consecutive. Hence each
low value occurs at most $4$ times per run, and at most $4(\rho+1)$ times in all.
Lemma~\ref{lem:mult} bounds the number $\ell$ of low increments by
$\ell^2\le8(\rho+1)\sum_jk_j\le8(\rho+1)\lfloor a/A\rfloor$. Let $s$ be the number of high
increments. If $s=0$, its bound is immediate. Otherwise let $h_*$ be their
smallest value. Then $sh_*\le\lfloor a/A\rfloor$ and
$a^3\le26244h_*^3A^4$, so $s^3a^3\le26244A^4(a/A)^3\le26244Aa^3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:rises}]
The rises inside a window are rises of the orbit, so $\rho+1\le R$. Combining the three parts
of Proposition~\ref{prop:rises}, as in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:generic}, gives for the
window $[2^k,2^{k+1})$ (when $6A>a$, part (2) gives $T-1\le a/A<6\le30A^{1/3}$)
\[
T_k\le 1+\sqrt R\,\min\bigl(\sqrt{8a}\,2^{-k/2},\ \sqrt2\,2^{k/2}\bigr)
+\min\bigl(a\,2^{-k},\ 30\cdot2^{k/3}\bigr).
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:geom} gives
$\sum_k\min(\sqrt{8a}\,2^{-k/2},\sqrt2\,2^{k/2})\ll(\sqrt{8a}\sqrt2)^{1/2}=2a^{1/4}$. It also gives
$\sum_k\min(a2^{-k},30\cdot2^{k/3})\ll a^{1/4}$. Since $\log_2a+1\ll a^{1/4}$, summing over $k$
gives the theorem.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:rises}, an orbit with $P(a,b)\asymp a^{3/10}$ must have $R\gg a^{1/10}$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:rises}(3) says more: inside the critical window $A\asymp a^{3/5}$, a
run of $T\asymp a^{3/10}$ remainders must contain $\gg a^{1/5}$ rises.
\end{remark}

\section{The Hankel hierarchy}\label{sec:hankel}

In this section and the next five we write $N=P(a,b)$, so that
$b_0>\dots>b_{N-1}>0=b_N$, and $k_i=q_{i+1}-q_i$. For each remaining tail we construct a probability measure
on its quotient differences. Reweighting by the reciprocal quotient will
make its total mass equal to $b_i/a$.

Put $p_i(j)=q_{i+j}-q_i$ for $0\le j<N-i$; the symbols $p_i,w_i$
are local to this construction. Define the weights backwards from
$w_{N-1}(0)=1$ by
\[
w_i(0)=1-\sum_{j=0}^{N-i-2}\frac{w_{i+1}(j)}{p_i(j+1)},\qquad
w_i(j+1)=\frac{w_{i+1}(j)}{p_i(j+1)}
\quad(0\le j<N-i-1).
\]
Let $\mu_i$ be the measure with mass $w_i(j)$ at $p_i(j)$, and let
\[
 d\nu_i(x)=\frac{d\mu_i(x)}{q_i+x},\qquad
 h_i(s)=\int x^s\,d\nu_i=\sum_{j=0}^{N-i-1}
 \frac{w_i(j)p_i(j)^s}{q_{i+j}}.
\]
The recursion is chosen to reproduce the division equation
$q_ih_i(0)+h_{i+1}(0)=1$ for $i+1<N$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:moments}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The weights are positive and sum to $1$.
\item $h_i(0)=b_i/a$, that is, $b_i/a=\mathbb E\bigl[1/(q_i+X_i)\bigr]$ for $X_i\sim\mu_i$; and
$h_{N-1}(s)=0$ for $s\ge1$.
\item $h_i(s+1)=\sum_{t\le s}\binom st k_i^{s-t}h_{i+1}(t)$ for $i+1<N$.
\item $h_i(s)=(-q_i)^sb_i/a+z$ with $z\in\mathbb Z$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(1) Since $p_i(j+1)\ge1$, the sum defining $w_i(0)$ is at most $1$. It is strictly less: if
$N-i-1\ge2$, the term $j=1$ has $p_i(2)\ge2$; if $i=N-2$, the last gap satisfies
$q_{N-1}\ge q_{N-2}+2$, because $b_{N-1}\mid a$ gives
$q_{N-1}-q_{N-2}-1=q_{N-2}(b_{N-2}-b_{N-1})/b_{N-1}\ge1$. (2) Since $p_i(j+1)=q_{i+j+1}-q_i$, the definitions give
$q_ih_i(0)+h_{i+1}(0)=1$, which is the normalised division equation; and
$h_{N-1}(0)=1/q_{N-1}=b_{N-1}/a$; since $\mu_{N-1}=\delta_0$, also $h_{N-1}(s)=0$ for $s\ge1$.
(3) Use $p_i(j+1)=k_i+p_{i+1}(j)$ and the binomial theorem.
(4) For $i=N-1$ this holds with $z=-(-q_{N-1})^sb_{N-1}/a=-(-1)^sq_{N-1}^{s-1}$ for $s\ge1$, since
$q_{N-1}b_{N-1}=a$. Then induct backwards on $i$ using (3), $b_{i+1}/a=1-q_ib_i/a$ and
$k_i-q_{i+1}=-q_i$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:hankel}
For $1\le r\le N-i$, the number $I_r(i)=a\det\bigl(h_i(j+l)\bigr)_{0\le j,l<r}$ is a positive
integer. Moreover $I_1(i)=b_i$, $I_2(i)=d_i$ (if $i+1<N$), $I_3(i)=D_i$ (if $i+3<N$), and
$I_4(i)=L_i$ (if $i+5<N$), where $D_i$ and $L_i$ are the curvature and the six-step integer
of the blocks starting at $b_i$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Integrality.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:moments}(4),
$h_i(s+1)+q_ih_i(s)\in\mathbb Z$. Multiply the Hankel matrix on the left by the unit lower
triangular matrix that adds $q_i$ times row $j-1$ to row $j$. This makes rows
$1,\dots,r-1$ integral and leaves the determinant unchanged. Expand along row $0$, whose entries
are $(-q_i)^lb_i/a+(\text{integer})$; then $a\det$ is an integer.

\emph{Positivity.} The Hankel matrix is the Gram matrix of $1,x,\dots,x^{r-1}$ in $L^2(\nu_i)$.
Its quadratic form is $\sum_jw_i(j)q_{i+j}^{-1}\bigl(\sum_lc_lp_i(j)^l\bigr)^2$. If this
vanishes, the polynomial $\sum_lc_lx^l$ vanishes at the $N-i\ge r$ distinct points $p_i(j)$, so
it is zero, by the invertibility of the Vandermonde matrix. So the matrix is positive definite.

\emph{The first four.} First, $I_1(i)=ah_i(0)=b_i$.
For $i+1<N$, write $\beta_j=b_{i+j}/a$. Then
$h_i(0)=\beta_0$, $h_i(1)=\beta_1$ and
$h_i(2)=k_i\beta_1+\beta_2$. Hence
\[
 I_2(i)=a\bigl(\beta_0(k_i\beta_1+\beta_2)-\beta_1^2\bigr)
       =a(\beta_0-\beta_1)=d_i,
\]
using $q_i\beta_0+\beta_1=q_{i+1}\beta_1+\beta_2=1$.
For the larger determinants, Lemma~\ref{lem:moments}(2),(3) makes the entries $h_i(s)$, $s\le2r-2$,
explicit polynomials in $\beta_j=b_{i+j}/a$ and $k_i,\dots,k_{i+2r-4}$; at the end of the orbit
this uses $h_{N-1}(s)=0$ for $s\ge1$, that is, $b_N=0$.
Expanding and reducing by $q_{i+j}\beta_j+\beta_{j+1}=1$ gives $D_i$
and $L_i$ in the stated ranges. The formal proof records polynomial
certificates for these two reductions.
\end{proof}

The ranges $i+3<N$ and $i+5<N$ cannot be relaxed to all $i$ at which $D_i$ or $L_i$ is defined.
For $a=926$ and $b=229$ the orbit is $229,10,6,2$, so $N=4$, and $I_3(1)=984$ while $D_1=988$.

\begin{remark}
In these index ranges, the positivity of $D$ and $L$, proved earlier by explicit identities, is an instance of the
positivity of Gram determinants. The integrality of $L$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:six}(1)) is an instance
of the integrality of $I_4$. A priori $I_r(i)$ involves the moments $h_i(s)$ for $s\le2r-2$,
and hence $b_i,\dots,b_{i+2r-2}$. In fact $I_3(i)$ depends only on $b_i,b_{i+1},b_{i+2}$ and
$q_i,q_{i+1},q_{i+2}$. Similarly $I_4(i)$ depends only on $b_i,\dots,b_{i+3}$ and
$q_i,\dots,q_{i+4}$: the later remainders cancel.
\end{remark}

\section{Breaking the exponent \texorpdfstring{$3/10$}{3/10}}\label{sec:2377}

In this section we prove Theorem~\ref{thm:2377}. We keep the notation of
Section~\ref{sec:hankel}: $b_0>b_1>\dots>b_{N-1}>0$ is the positive part of the orbit,
$N=P(a,b)$, and $q_i=\lfloor a/b_i\rfloor$.

\subsection{Heuristics}
The exponent $3/10$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} comes from a single balanced configuration:
$T\asymp a^{3/10}$ remainders in a window $[A,2A)$ with $A\asymp a^{3/5}$, quotients
$q\asymp a^{2/5}$ and typical increments $k\asymp a^{1/10}$. At this scale the four-term Hankel
invariant $I_4(i)=L(i)$ has size $\asymp aH^6/q^4\asymp 1$, where $H$ bounds the increments.
The idea is that a positive integer of bounded size carries information. The labels
$I_4(i),I_4(i+1),I_4(i+2)$, together with four further integers of the same size, determine a
\emph{pattern}. States with a given pattern satisfy two linear equations and one quadratic
equation in five consecutive increments. So a pattern accounts for at most $O(H^2)$
increment tuples, and each tuple for at most four states. At the balanced point this
gives $T\ll H^2\asymp a^{1/5}$, a large saving. Moving away from the balanced point costs a
power of the pattern size, and optimising gives the exponent $23/77$.

\subsection{The pattern matrix}
Fix a state $i$ with $i+6<N$ and write, for $m\ge0$,
\begin{gather*}
k_m=q_{i+m+1}-q_{i+m},\qquad d_m=b_{i+m}-b_{i+m+1},\\
s_m=k_0+\dots+k_{m-1},\qquad r_m=k_m(s_m+s_{m+1})+k_{m+1}.
\end{gather*}
All indices here are local to the chosen state, and the definitions apply
whenever the indicated orbit entries exist; the empty sum gives $s_0=0$.
So $s_m=q_{i+m}-q_i$, $r_0=k_0^2+k_1$, $r_1=k_1(2k_0+k_1)+k_2$, and so on. Consider the
$3\times4$ integer matrix
\[
\mathcal M_i=\begin{pmatrix} d_0&d_1&d_2&d_3\\ k_0&k_1&k_2&k_3\\ r_0&r_1&r_2&r_3\end{pmatrix},
\]
and let $U_m(i)=(-1)^{m+1}\det(\mathcal M_i\text{ with column $m$ deleted})$ for
$0\le m\le3$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:pattern}
\begin{enumerate}
\item $U_3(i)=L(i)=I_4(i)$ and $U_0(i)=-L(i+1)=-I_4(i+1)$, where $L$ is the six-step integer of
Lemma~\ref{lem:six}.
\item $\sum_m U_m(i)\,d_m=\sum_m U_m(i)\,k_m=\sum_m U_m(i)\,r_m=0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(2) is the Laplace expansion of a $4\times4$ determinant with a repeated row. For (1), the
identity $\det(\text{columns }0,1,2)=L(i)$ holds as a polynomial identity in
$d_0,d_1,d_2,k_0,\dots,k_3$, by direct expansion; and $L(i)=I_4(i)$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:hankel}. Passing from $i$ to $i+1$ shifts the $d$- and $k$-rows by one
column, and, since $s_m(i+1)=s_{m+1}(i)-k_0$, it replaces $r_m$ by $r_{m+1}-2k_0k_{m+1}$. This
row operation does not change the determinant, so $\det(\text{columns }1,2,3)=L(i+1)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The vector $C_m=U_m/U_3$ has a meaning: $\pi_i=\sum_{m\le3}C_m(F_{m+1}-F_m)$, with Newton
polynomials $F_m(x)=\prod_{l<m}(x-s_l)$, is the monic orthogonal polynomial of degree four for
the measure $\nu_i$ of Section~\ref{sec:hankel}. The three rows of $\mathcal M_i$ encode
$\nu_i(\pi_i)=0$ and $\mu_i(x\pi_i)=\mu_i(x^2\pi_i)=0$, and $\nu_i(xf)=\mu_i(f)-q_i\nu_i(f)$ turns these
into $\nu_i(x^j\pi_i)=0$ for $j\le3$. In particular $U_m(i)/I_4(i)$ are tail sums of the
Newton coefficients of $\pi_i$: writing $\pi_i=\sum_{l\le4}\gamma_lF_l$, one has $U_m(i)/I_4(i)=\gamma_{m+1}+\dots+\gamma_4$,
and $\gamma_0=-C_0=I_4(i+1)/I_4(i)$. This interpretation is not needed for
the elementary count in this section; it will be used in Section~\ref{sec:joint}.
\end{remark}

The \emph{pattern} of $i$ is the vector of seven integers
\[
\theta(i)=\bigl(L(i),\,L(i+1),\,L(i+2),\,U_1(i),\,U_2(i),\,U_1(i+1),\,U_2(i+1)\bigr),
\]
and its \emph{gaps} are $\kappa(i)=(k_0,\dots,k_4)$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:pattern},
$\theta(i)$ determines $U(i)$ and $U(i+1)$ completely.

\subsection{Three consequences}

\begin{lemma}[The reduced quadratic]\label{lem:quad}
Write $\theta(i)=(N_0,N_1,N_2,u_1,u_2,v_1,v_2)$ and $\kappa(i)=(k_0,\dots,k_4)$. Then
\[
N_1N_0(N_1+N_0)\,k_3^2+\beta\,k_3+\gamma=0,
\]
where $\beta$ and $\gamma$ are polynomials in $\theta(i)$, $k_1$ and $k_2$ alone.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Lemma~\ref{lem:pattern}(2) for the $k$-row at $i$ and at $i+1$ gives
\[
N_1k_0=u_1k_1+u_2k_2+N_0k_3,\qquad N_1k_4=N_2k_1-v_1k_2-v_2k_3 .
\]
Substitute these into $N_1^2\sum_mU_m(i)r_m=0$. Since $k_0$ enters the $r_m$ at most
quadratically and $k_4$ only linearly, the result is a polynomial in $(\theta,k_1,k_2,k_3)$.
The coefficient of $k_3^2$ comes from $-N_1\cdot N_1^2k_0^2$, from $N_0\cdot N_1^2\cdot 2k_0k_3$ and
from $N_0\cdot N_1^2k_3^2$, and it equals
$-N_1N_0^2+2N_1N_0^2+N_1^2N_0=N_1N_0(N_1+N_0)$. Explicitly
\begin{align*}
\beta&=2N_1^2N_0(k_1+k_2)+N_1^2u_2+2N_1N_0(u_1k_1+u_2k_2)-N_1N_0v_2,\\
\gamma&=N_1(u_1k_1+u_2k_2)^2+N_1^2\bigl(u_1k_1^2+2u_2k_1k_2+u_2k_2^2+u_1k_2-N_1k_1\bigr)
+N_0N_1(N_2k_1-v_1k_2).
\end{align*}
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The multiplicity identity]\label{lem:multid}
Let $F_m(Y)=\prod_{l<m}(Y-s_l)$ and $\mathcal Q_i(Y)=\sum_{m=0}^{3}U_m(i)\,F_m(Y)\,(Y-s_m-1)$.
Then
\[
\mathcal Q_i(-q_i)=a\,H_4(k_0,\dots,k_4),
\]
where $H_4$ is an explicit polynomial with $43$ terms, independent of $i$. As a polynomial in
$q$, $\mathcal Q_i(-q)$ has degree four and leading coefficient $U_3(i)=L(i)\ge1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The division equations read $b_{i+m+1}=a-(q_i+s_m)b_{i+m}$. Use them to eliminate
$b_{i+1},\dots,b_{i+4}$ from both sides. The difference becomes the zero polynomial in
$a,q_i,b_i,k_0,\dots,k_4$; explicit certificates are in the Lean proof. The polynomial $H_4$ is
the Hankel determinant $\det\bigl(\mu_i(x^{j+l})\bigr)_{j,l<4}$, and the identity is
$I_4(i)\,\pi_i(-q_i)=a\det\bigl(\mu_i(x^{j+l})\bigr)$. This follows from the determinant formula for
$\pi_i$, since $\nu_i(x^{s+1})+q_i\nu_i(x^s)=\mu_i(x^s)$. The
leading coefficient comes from the term $m=3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Labels]\label{lem:labels}
Let $i+6<N$ and put
\[
G(k_0,\dots,k_4)=(k_0+1)\bigl(k_1s_2+s_2+k_1+k_2+1\bigr)
\bigl(k_2(k_1+k_2)s_3+e_3\bigr),
\]
where $e_3=s_3(k_1+k_2)+s_3k_2+(k_1+k_2)k_2+(k_0+2k_1+3k_2)(k_3+1)+k_3^2+2k_3+k_4+1$. Then
$L(i)\,q_i^4\le a\,G(k_0,\dots,k_4)$. If moreover $b_i<2A$, then
$L(i)\,a^3\le 256A^4\,G(k_0,\dots,k_4)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The Newton polynomials $F_0,\dots,F_3$ are monic of degrees $0,\dots,3$. So the matrix
$\bigl(\nu_i(F_jF_l)\bigr)$ is $\mathcal U\mathcal H\mathcal U^{\mathsf T}$, where $\mathcal H$ is the Hankel matrix of $\nu_i$ and $\mathcal U$ is unit
lower triangular. Hence it has determinant $I_4(i)/a$, and it is positive definite, as in the
proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:hankel}. By Hadamard's inequality,
\[
\frac{I_4(i)}a\le\prod_{j<4}\nu_i(F_j^2)\le\prod_{j<4}\frac{\mu_i(F_j^2)}{q_i},
\]
since $\nu_i=\mu_i/(q_i+x)\le\mu_i/q_i$ on the nonnegative support. By the shift rule
$\mu_i(F_jg)=\mu_{i+j}\bigl(g(x+s_j)\bigr)$ and the moments $\mu_{i+m}(1)=\mu_{i+m}(x)=1$,
$\mu_{i+m}(x^2)=k_m+1$, $\mu_{i+m}(x^3)=k_m^2+2k_m+k_{m+1}+1$, the product equals $G$. For the second
claim, $a=q_ib_i+b_{i+1}<2A(q_i+1)\le4Aq_i$, so $L(i)a^4\le256A^4L(i)q_i^4\le 256A^4aG$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The size of a pattern}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:psize}
Let $1\le k_0,\dots,k_5\le H$ and $A\ge1$. Suppose $N_0=L(i)$ and $N_1=L(i+1)$ satisfy
$N_0a^3\le256A^4G(k_0,\dots,k_4)$ and $N_1a^3\le256A^4G(k_1,\dots,k_5)$, and that the
\emph{scale condition}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scale}
237568\,A^4H^5\le a^3
\end{equation}
holds. Then $L(i)$, $L(i+1)$, $|U_1(i)|$ and $|U_2(i)|$ are all at most $172032\,A^4H^6/a^3$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $V=k_1k_2(k_1+k_2)$ and $\Delta_4=k_0\cdot k_1s_2\cdot k_2(k_1+k_2)s_3$. Expanding $G$ and
bounding each of the three error terms by $56H^5$, $170H^5$ and $238H^5$ gives
$G(k_0,\dots,k_4)\le\Delta_4+464H^5$. Also $\Delta_4\le 6H^3V$, because
$\Delta_4=k_0s_2s_3\cdot V$. Similarly $G(k_1,\dots,k_5)\le 6H^3V+464H^5$, because the
corresponding product is $V\cdot k_3(k_2+k_3)(k_1+k_2+k_3)$. By~\eqref{eq:scale} and $N_0,N_1\ge1$,
$256A^4\cdot464H^5\le a^3/2\le N_ja^3/2$ for $j=0,1$, so
\[
N_0a^3,\ N_1a^3\le 3072\,A^4H^3V .
\]
Since $N_0\ge1$, this and~\eqref{eq:scale} give $V\ge 77H^2$.

Now solve for $(u_1,u_2)=(U_1(i),U_2(i))$ from the $k$- and $r$-relations of
Lemma~\ref{lem:pattern}. These read $u_1k_1+u_2k_2=N_1k_0-N_0k_3$ and
$u_1r_1+u_2r_2=N_1r_0-N_0r_3$. The determinant is
\[
\delta=k_1r_2-k_2r_1=k_1k_2(k_1+k_2)+k_1k_3-k_2^2\ \ge\ V-H^2\ \ge\ V/2 .
\]
Using $r_m\le (2m+2)H^2$, Cramer's rule gives $|u_j|\delta\le 14(N_0+N_1)H^3$, hence
\[
|u_j|\,V a^3\le 28H^3(N_0+N_1)a^3\le 28H^3\cdot 6144A^4H^3V ,
\]
that is, $|u_j|a^3\le172032A^4H^6$. Finally $N_ja^3\le3072A^4H^3V\le6144A^4H^6$, since
$V\le2H^3$.
\end{proof}

The point of the argument is in the first paragraph. The two adjacent labels share the
Vandermonde factor $V$, so the ratio $L(i+1)/L(i)$ never enters the bound.

\subsection{The counting lemma}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:pcount}
Let $\mathcal G$ be a set of states $i$ with $i+8<N$. Suppose that for each $i\in\mathcal G$
all entries of $\theta(i)$ have absolute value at most $M$, and all entries of $\kappa(i)$ lie
in $[1,H]$. Then $|\mathcal G|\le 8(2M+1)^7H^2$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\emph{(i) At most four states per $(\theta,\kappa)$.} If $\theta(i)=\theta$ and
$\kappa(i)=\kappa$, then by Lemma~\ref{lem:multid} $q_i$ is a root of the polynomial
$\mathcal Q(-q)-aH_4(\kappa)$. This polynomial depends only on $(\theta,\kappa)$, and it has
degree four and leading coefficient $\theta_0\ge1$. Since $q_i$ is strictly increasing in $i$,
there are at most four such states.

\emph{(ii) At most two values of $\kappa$ per $(\theta,k_1,k_2)$.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:quad},
$k_3$ is a root of a quadratic whose coefficients depend only on $(\theta,k_1,k_2)$. The
leading coefficient is $N_1N_0(N_1+N_0)>0$. The two linear relations then determine $k_0$ and
$k_4$, because $N_1\ne0$.

\emph{(iii)} There are at most $(2M+1)^7H^2$ values of $(\theta,k_1,k_2)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The window bound}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:twostate}
Let $b_s,\dots,b_{s+T-1}$ be a window run in $[A,2A)$ with $T\ge36$, and put
$H=\lfloor 28a/(AT)\rfloor+1$ and $M=\lfloor172032A^4H^6/a^3\rfloor$. If~\eqref{eq:scale}
holds, then
\[
T\le 16\,(2M+1)^7H^2 .
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Call a start $i$ \emph{good} if $s\le i$, $i+9\le s+T$ and $q_{j+1}-q_j\le H$ for $i\le j\le i+6$,
that is, $k_0,\dots,k_6\le H$ at state $i$.

\emph{Most starts are good.} Let $\mathcal B$ be the set of $j\in[s,s+T-1)$ with $q_{j+1}-q_j>H$. Then
$|\mathcal B|(H+1)\le q_{s+T-1}-q_s\le a/A$. Since $H\cdot AT>28a$, this gives
$28|\mathcal B|\le T$. Each $j\in\mathcal B$ spoils at most seven starts, so at least
$T-9-7|\mathcal B|\ge T/2$ starts are good, as $T\ge36$.

\emph{Good starts satisfy Proposition~\ref{prop:pcount}.} Let $i$ be good. Then $i+8<N$, and
$b_i,b_{i+1},b_{i+2}<2A$. So Lemma~\ref{lem:labels} applies at $i$, $i+1$ and $i+2$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:psize} at $i$ and at $i+1$ bounds all seven entries of $\theta(i)$ by $M$.
Therefore $T/2\le 8(2M+1)^7H^2$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:2377}}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:77}
Let $T\ge36$ be the length of a window run in $[A,2A)$. Then $T^{77}\le C_{77}\Delta^{22}a^{23}$
for an absolute constant $C_{77}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We prove this with
\[
C_{77}=122449^{20}+(8\cdot237568\cdot56^5)^{11}+(64\cdot50176)^{11}
+8^{16}\cdot16\cdot3^7\cdot172032^7\cdot56^{44}.
\]
We use Proposition~\ref{prop:six}, $T^6A^2\le96\cdot10^6a^3$, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:drop}, which gives $T^2\le 2T(T-1)\le 8A\Delta$.

\emph{Case $AT>28a$.} Then $784a^2T^4\le T^6A^2\le96\cdot10^6a^3$, so $T^4\le122449\,a$ and
$T^{77}\le 122449^{20}a^{20}$.

From now on $AT\le28a$, so $H\cdot AT\le 28a+AT\le 56a$.

\emph{Case~\eqref{eq:scale} fails.} Then $a^3(AT)^5<237568A^4(H\,AT)^5\le 237568\cdot56^5A^4a^5$,
so $AT^5\le237568\cdot56^5a^2$. Hence $T^7=T^5T^2\le 8\Delta\,AT^5\le 8\cdot237568\cdot56^5\,\Delta a^2$.
Raise this to the eleventh power.

\emph{Case~\eqref{eq:scale} holds and $M=0$.} Then $T\le16H^2$, so
$T(AT)^2\le16(56a)^2$. Hence $T^7=T^3(T^2)^2\le 64\Delta^2\,T^3A^2\le 64\cdot50176\,\Delta^2a^2$.
This case is in fact vacuous: the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:twostate} provides a good
start $i$, and there $1\le L(i)\le M$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:psize}. We treat it anyway, here and
in the analogous proofs below, so that the constants match the formal proof.

\emph{Case~\eqref{eq:scale} holds and $M\ge1$.} Then $2M+1\le3M$ and $Ma^3\le172032A^4H^6$, so
\[
T\,a^{21}\le 16\cdot3^7\,(Ma^3)^7H^2\le 16\cdot3^7\cdot172032^7\,A^{28}H^{44}.
\]
Multiplying by $(AT)^{44}$ and using $H\cdot AT\le56a$ gives
$T^{45}A^{16}\le 16\cdot3^7\cdot172032^7\cdot56^{44}\,a^{23}$. Finally
\[
T^{77}=T^{45}(T^2)^{16}\le 8^{16}\Delta^{16}\,T^{45}A^{16}
\le 8^{16}\cdot16\cdot3^7\cdot172032^7\cdot56^{44}\,\Delta^{16}a^{23}.
\]
In every case $T^{77}\le C_{77}\Delta^{22}a^{23}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:2377}]
This is the argument of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, with Proposition~\ref{prop:77} in place of
Corollary~\ref{cor:window}. Let $\varepsilon>0$ and choose an integer $m>1/\varepsilon$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:div}, $\Delta^{22m}\le C_{22m}\cdot32a^7$, so every dyadic window has
$T\le35$ or
\[
T^{77m}\le C_{77}^m\,C_{22m}\,32\;a^{23m+7}.
\]
Since $(23m+7)/(77m)=23/77+1/(11m)\le23/77+\varepsilon/2$, each of the $\lfloor\log_2a\rfloor+1$
windows has $T\ll_\varepsilon a^{23/77+\varepsilon/2}$, and the theorem follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:2377}
In terms of the scales, Proposition~\ref{prop:twostate} says $T\ll X^7H^2$, where
$X=A^4H^6/a^3$ bounds the seven pattern integers and $H\asymp a/(AT)$. Thus
$X\asymp a^3/(A^2T^6)$ and $T^{45}A^{16}\ll a^{23}$. The drop bound $A\gg T^2/\Delta$ then gives
$T^{77}\ll\Delta^{16}a^{23}$. The scale condition~\eqref{eq:scale} says $Q^4\gg aH^5$, where
$B=a/A$, and its failure gives the stronger bound $T\ll a^{2/7+o(1)}$.
\end{remark}

\section{The ternary quadratic and the exponent \texorpdfstring{$11/37$}{11/37}}\label{sec:1137}

Proposition~\ref{prop:pcount} uses only one coefficient of the reduced quadratic. Here we use
all of it, which saves a factor $H$ in the count per pattern and proves
Theorem~\ref{thm:1137}. We keep the notation of Section~\ref{sec:2377}, and, as in
Lemma~\ref{lem:quad}, write a pattern as
$\theta=(N_0,N_1,N_2,u_1,u_2,v_1,v_2)=(L(i),L(i+1),L(i+2),U_1(i),U_2(i),U_1(i+1),U_2(i+1))$.
In particular $A$ is still the scale of the window $[A,2A)$.

\subsection{Signs}
Throughout this subsection the pattern and gaps arise from the same state,
so the cofactor relations of Lemma~\ref{lem:pattern} remain in force.
Put $S(a,b,c)=a^2b+(a-1)b^2+ac$ and $E(a,b,c,d)=a^2c+(2a-1)bc+ac^2+ad$. Both are positive at
positive integers.

\begin{lemma}[Signs]\label{lem:signs}
If $N_0,N_1\ge1$ and $k_0,\dots,k_4\ge1$, then $u_1>0>u_2$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Solving the $k$- and $r$-relations of Lemma~\ref{lem:pattern} for $(u_1,u_2)$ by Cramer's rule
gives the exact identities
\begin{align*}
u_1S(k_1,k_2,k_3)&=N_1E(k_0,k_1,k_2,k_3)+N_0S(k_2,k_3,k_4),\\
-u_2S(k_1,k_2,k_3)&=N_1S(k_0,k_1,k_2)+N_0E(k_1,k_2,k_3,k_4),
\end{align*}
since the Cramer determinant is $k_1r_2-k_2r_1=S(k_1,k_2,k_3)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The ternary relation}
For a pattern $\theta$ let
\begin{align*}
F_\theta(x,y,z)={}&(u_1x+u_2y+N_0z)^2+N_1u_1x^2+N_1u_2y^2+N_0N_1z^2+2N_1u_2xy+2N_0N_1xz\\
&+2N_0N_1yz+(N_0N_2-N_1^2)x+(N_1u_1-N_0v_1)y+(N_1u_2-N_0v_2)z .
\end{align*}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:tern}
At every state $i$ with $i+7<N$, $F_{\theta(i)}(k_1,k_2,k_3)=0$. Write $F_\theta=\alpha x^2+\beta xy+\gamma
y^2+d(z)x+e(z)y+f(z)$. Then $\alpha=u_1(u_1+N_1)$,
$\beta^2-4\alpha\gamma=-4N_1u_2(N_1+u_1)(u_1-u_2)$, and
\[
(\beta d_1-2\alpha e_1)^2+(\beta^2-4\alpha\gamma)(4\alpha f_2-d_1^2)
=16\,N_0N_1^2u_1(N_0-u_2)(N_1+u_1)^2(u_1-u_2),
\]
where $d_1,e_1,f_2$ are the coefficients of $z,z,z^2$ in $d,e,f$, respectively.
In particular, under the
hypotheses of Lemma~\ref{lem:signs}, $\alpha>0$, $\beta^2-4\alpha\gamma>0$ and the last
quantity is nonzero.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The identity
$N_1F_\theta(k_1,k_2,k_3)=N_1^2R+(N_0N_1k_3-N_1^2k_0+N_1u_1k_1+N_1u_2k_2)K_0-N_0N_1K_1$, where $K_0$, $K_1$
and $R$ are the $k$-relations at $i$, $i+1$ and the $r$-relation at $i$, gives the first claim.
The rest is expansion. So $F_\theta\equiv N_1R$ modulo the two linear relations.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Norm equations}

\begin{lemma}[Norm equations]\label{lem:norm}
Let $D\ge1$, $m\ne0$, $B\ge1$ be integers and $Z=B(\lfloor\sqrt D\rfloor+2)$. The number of
$(x,y)\in[-B,B]^2$ with $x^2-Dy^2=m$ is at most $2\tau(|m|)^3(\log_3Z^2+1)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $D=h^2$ then $(x-hy)(x+hy)=m$, and $x-hy$ is one of the $2\tau(|m|)$ signed divisors of $m$;
it determines $(x,y)$. Otherwise write $D=df^2$ with $d>1$ squarefree, so $d$ is not a square
and $\sqrt d$ is irrational. For a solution put $g=\gcd(x,fy)$, $u=x/g$, $w=fy/g$ and
$n=u^2-dw^2$, so that $m=g^2n$. Since $\gcd(u,w)=1$ and $n\equiv u^2\pmod w$, $w$ is invertible
modulo $n$. Let $t\in[0,|n|)$ be the residue of $uw^{-1}$; then $t^2\equiv d$ and $u\equiv tw$
modulo $n$.

\emph{The residues.} The number of square roots of $d$ modulo $c\ge1$ is at most $\tau(c)^2$. Modulo
$p^e$ it is at most four. If $p\mid d$, either $e=1$ and the only root is $0$, or $e\ge2$ and a
root would force $p^2\mid d$. If $p\nmid d$ is odd, two roots $r,s$ satisfy
$p^e\mid(s-r)(s+r)$, and $p$ cannot divide both factors, so $s\equiv\pm r$. If $p=2\nmid d$,
then $s,r$ are odd, one of $(s\mp r)/2$ is odd, and $s\equiv\pm r\pmod{2^{e-1}}$. The count is
submultiplicative over coprime factors, by the Chinese remainder theorem, and $4\le\tau(p^e)^2$.

\emph{A class.} Fix $g$ (with $g^2\mid m$), $t$ and the sign of $\xi=u+w\sqrt d$. Two
solutions $(u,w),(u',w')$ in this class satisfy $n\mid uu'-dww'$ and $n\mid wu'-uw'$. So
$\xi=\xi'\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon=r+s\sqrt d$, where $r=(uu'-dww')/n$, $s=(wu'-uw')/n$ and
$r^2-ds^2=1$. If $|\xi|>|\xi'|$ then $\varepsilon>1$, and then $\varepsilon\ge3$: indeed
$\varepsilon^{-1}=r-s\sqrt d\in(0,1)$, so $2r=\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{-1}>2$, hence $r\ge2$, and
$\varepsilon<3$ would give $\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{-1}<4$. Now $|u|+|w|\sqrt d\le|x|+|y|\sqrt
D\le Z$, so $|\xi|,|\bar\xi|\le Z$, and $|\xi\bar\xi|=|n|\ge1$ gives $Z|\xi|\ge1$. Hence the map
$\xi\mapsto\lfloor\log_3(Z|\xi|)\rfloor$ is injective on the class, with values in
$[0,\log_3Z^2]$.

There are at most $\tau(|m|)$ values of $g$, $\tau(|m|)^2$ of $t$ and two signs.
\end{proof}

The argument is the classical one; compare Vaughan and Wooley~\cite[Lemma~3.5]{VW}.

\subsection{Slices}

\begin{lemma}[Slices]\label{lem:slice}
Let $F(x,y,z)=\alpha x^2+\beta xy+\gamma y^2+(d_1z+d_0)x+(e_1z+e_0)y+f_2z^2+f_1z+f_0$ have integer
coefficients bounded by $K$, with $\alpha>0$, $D=\beta^2-4\alpha\gamma>0$ and
$\ell=(\beta d_1-2\alpha e_1)^2+D(4\alpha f_2-d_1^2)\ne0$. Let $H\ge1$, and suppose that
$2\tau(|m|)^3(\log_3\bigl((11K^2H(3K+2))^2\bigr)+1)\le \omega$ for all integers $0<|m|\le116K^4H^2$. Then $F$ has at most
$(2H+1)(\omega+4)$ zeros in $[-H,H]^3$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $t=2\alpha x+\beta y+d(z)$, $\Lambda(z)=\beta d(z)-2\alpha e(z)$, $s=Dy+\Lambda(z)$ and
$\mathcal N(z)=\Lambda(z)^2+D(4\alpha f(z)-d(z)^2)$. Completing squares gives
$s^2-Dt^2=\mathcal N(z)-4\alpha DF$. In the box, $|s|,|t|\le11K^2H$,
$\sqrt D\le3K$ and $|\mathcal N(z)|\le116K^4H^2$. For fixed $z$ the map $(x,y)\mapsto(s,t)$
is injective. So if $\mathcal N(z)\ne0$ the slice has at most $\omega$ zeros, by
Lemma~\ref{lem:norm}. The $z^2$-coefficient of $\mathcal N$ is $\ell\ne0$, so at most two
slices have $\mathcal N(z)=0$. In such a slice $s^2=Dt^2$, which for fixed $y$ is a
quadratic in $x$ with leading coefficient $4\alpha^2D\ne0$, so it has at most
$2(2H+1)$ zeros.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The count}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:pcount2}
Under the hypotheses of Proposition~\ref{prop:pcount}, let $K=4M^2$, and suppose $\omega$ satisfies
the hypothesis of Lemma~\ref{lem:slice}. Then $|\mathcal G|\le4(2M+1)^7(2H+1)(\omega+4)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
As before, at most four states share $(\theta,\kappa)$, and $\kappa$ is determined by
$(\theta,k_1,k_2,k_3)$. For a fixed $\theta$, the triples $(k_1,k_2,k_3)$ are zeros of
$F_\theta$ in $[1,H]^3$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:tern}). The coefficients of $F_\theta$ are at most
$4M^2$, and Lemmas~\ref{lem:signs} and~\ref{lem:tern} give the nondegeneracy hypotheses of
Lemma~\ref{lem:slice}.
\end{proof}

Let $P_1(a)=10^{300}a^{100}$, let $\tau^*(X)=\max_{n\le X}\tau(n)$ with the convention
$\tau(0)=0$, and let $\Xi(a)=2\tau^*(P_1(a))^3(\log_3P_1(a)+1)+4$. In every window
$H\le29a$ and $M\le10^{16}a^7$; the same bound holds for the larger $M$ of
Proposition~\ref{prop:twostate3} below. Hence $K=4M^2$ satisfies
$116K^4H^2\le P_1(a)$ and $(11K^2H(3K+2))^2\le P_1(a)$, so $\omega=\Xi(a)-4$ satisfies the
hypothesis of Lemma~\ref{lem:slice}. The argument of Proposition~\ref{prop:twostate} then gives:

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:twostate2}
In the setting of Proposition~\ref{prop:twostate}, if~\eqref{eq:scale} holds then
$T\le 24(2M+1)^7H\,\Xi(a)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:74}
For every window run with $T\ge36$, $T^{74}\le C_{74}\Delta^{19}\Xi(a)^{19}a^{22}$ for an
absolute constant $C_{74}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We prove this with
$C_{74}=122449^{19}+(8\cdot237568\cdot56^5)^{11}+10752^{19}+8^{15}\cdot24\cdot3^7\cdot172032^7\cdot56^{43}$.
The four cases of Proposition~\ref{prop:77} carry over. If $AT>28a$, then $T^4\le122449a$. If
the scale condition fails, then $T^7\le8\cdot237568\cdot56^5\Delta a^2$. If $M=0$, then
$T^2A\le1344\,\Xi a$ and $T^4\le10752\,\Delta\Xi a$. If $M\ge1$, then
$Ta^{21}\le24\cdot3^7\cdot172032^7A^{28}H^{43}\Xi$, so $T^{44}A^{15}\le
24\cdot3^7\cdot172032^7\cdot56^{43}\,\Xi a^{22}$, and $T^{74}=T^{44}(T^2)^{15}\le(8A\Delta)^{15}T^{44}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:1137}]
By the divisor bound $\tau(n)^k\le C_kn$, both $\Delta$ and $\tau^*(P_1(a))$ are
$O_\eta(a^\eta)$, and $\log_3P_1(a)\ll_\eta a^\eta$. So $\Xi(a)\ll_\eta a^\eta$, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:74} gives $T\ll_\eta a^{(22+38\eta)/74}$ for every dyadic window. Since
$22/74=11/37$, summing over the $O(\log a)$ windows proves the theorem.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
In terms of the scales, the count per pattern is now $O(Ha^\eta)$ instead of $O(H^2)$. So
$T\ll a^\eta X^7H$, which gives $T^{44}\ll a^{7+\eta}B^{15}$ with $B=a/A$, and with $B\ll a^{1+\eta}/T^2$ the
bound $T^{74}\ll a^{22+O(\eta)}$. At the critical point $T\approx a^{11/37}$, $B\approx
a^{15/37}$, $H\approx a^{4/37}$ and $X\approx a^{1/37}$.
\end{remark}

\section{Ten minors and the exponent \texorpdfstring{$8/27$}{8/27}}\label{sec:827}

The seven pattern integers of Section~\ref{sec:1137} were counted as if they were independent.
They are not: they are coordinates of a single vector of $3\times3$ minors. Counting such vectors
directly saves a factor $X^2$ and proves Theorem~\ref{thm:827}.

\subsection{The full minor vector}
Let $\mathcal M_i$ now be the $3\times5$ matrix with rows $(d_0,\dots,d_4)$, $(k_0,\dots,k_4)$
and $(r_0,\dots,r_4)$, and let $p_{c_1c_2c_3}$ be its minor on columns $c_1<c_2<c_3$. Expanding
the cofactors of Section~\ref{sec:2377} gives
\[
\theta(i)=(p_{012},\,p_{123},\,p_{234},\,p_{023},\,-p_{013},\,p_{134},\,-p_{124}).
\]

\begin{lemma}[Expansion]\label{lem:expand}
Let $q=q_i$ and $s_j=q_{i+j}-q$. If $i+j+3<N$ then
\[
\frac{b_{i+j}}a=\frac1q-\frac{1+s_j}{q^2}+\frac{s_j^2+2s_j+k_j+1}{q^3}-e_j,\qquad
0\le e_j\le\frac{\mu_{i+j}\bigl((x+s_j)^3\bigr)}{q^4}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Apply $\mu_{i+j}$ to $\frac1{q+y}=\frac1q-\frac y{q^2}+\frac{y^2}{q^3}-\frac{y^3}{q^3(q+y)}$ with
$y=s_j+x\ge0$, using $b_{i+j}/a=\nu_{i+j}(1)=\mu_{i+j}\bigl(1/(q+s_j+x)\bigr)$, $\mu(1)=\mu(x)=1$
and $\mu(x^2)=k+1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[All minors]\label{prop:allminors}
Let $i+8<N$, $b_i<2A$ and $k_0,\dots,k_6\le H$ at state $i$. Then every minor of $\mathcal M_i$
satisfies $|p|\,a^3\le3609600\,A^4H^6$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Subtracting the expansions for $j$ and $j+1$ gives
$d_j/a=k_j/q^2-(r_j+k_j)/q^3+e_{j+1}-e_j$. So replacing the first row by
$d'_j=d_j-a(q^{-2}-q^{-3})k_j+aq^{-3}r_j=a(e_{j+1}-e_j)$ changes no minor. Now
$|d'_j|\le235aH^3/q^4$, $|k_j|\le H$ and $|r_j|\le10H^2$, so $|p|\le14100\,aH^6/q^4$. Finally
$a\le4Aq$.
\end{proof}

This replaces Lemma~\ref{lem:psize}, and the scale condition~\eqref{eq:scale} is no longer
needed.

\subsection{Counting minor vectors}

\begin{proposition}[Minor vectors]\label{prop:minorvec}
The number of vectors of minors of integer $3\times5$ matrices with all coordinates in $[-Y,Y]$ is
at most $1+20\sum_{m\le Y}729\,m^4\tau(m)\ll Y^{5+o(1)}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For a nonzero vector $p$ choose a pivot $\pi$ with $|p_\pi|=|m|$ maximal, and let $c_4,c_5$ be
the two columns outside $\pi$. Let $u_a$ ($v_a$) be the minor with the $a$-th pivot column replaced
by $c_4$ ($c_5$), with the sign of the column permutation. By Cramer's rule and the Pl\"ucker
relations, the remaining three minors satisfy $m\,p_{w_a}=\pm(u\times v)_a$ (thirty polynomial
identities, checked symbolically). So $p$ is determined by $(\pi,\operatorname{sgn}m,|m|,u,v)$,
where $|u_a|,|v_a|\le|m|$ and $|m|$ divides every coordinate of $u\times v$.

Fix $m\ge1$ and $u$, and let $g=\gcd(m,u_1,u_2,u_3)$, $n=m/g$, $u=gu_0$. By B\'ezout there are
$c\in\mathbb Z^3$ and $x\in\mathbb Z$ with $c\cdot u_0+xn=1$. If $m\mid u\times v$, then
$n\mid u_0\times v$, and the identity
$v-(c\cdot v)u_0=xn\,v+c\times(v\times u_0)$ shows $v\equiv(c\cdot v)u_0\pmod n$. Hence $v$ lies
in one of $n$ classes modulo $n$, each meeting $[-m,m]^3$ in at most $(3g)^3$ points: at most
$27mg^2$ choices. Summing over $u$, grouped by $g=d\mid m$, gives
$\sum_u g^2\le\sum_{d\mid m}d^2(3m/d)^3\le27m^3\tau(m)$, so at most $729m^4\tau(m)$ pairs
$(u,v)$. There are ten pivots and two signs.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The bound}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:twostate3}
For a window run in $[A,2A)$ with $T\ge36$, let $H=\lfloor28a/(AT)\rfloor+1$ and
$M=\lfloor3609600A^4H^6/a^3\rfloor$. Then $T\ll(M+1)^5H\,\Xi(a)^2$ and
\[
T^{54}\ll\Delta^{14}\Xi(a)^{28}a^{16}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Explicitly, we prove $T\le24\cdot14581\,(M+1)^5H\,\Xi(a)^2$ and $T^{54}\le C_{54}\Delta^{14}\Xi(a)^{28}a^{16}$
with $C_{54}=122449^{14}+(448\cdot24\cdot14581)^{14}+8^{11}\cdot32\cdot24\cdot14581\cdot3609600^5\cdot56^{31}$.
As in Propositions~\ref{prop:twostate} and~\ref{prop:pcount2}, at least $T/2$ starts are good, at
most four good starts share $(\theta,\kappa)$, and there are at most $(2H+1)(\omega+4)$ gap triples per
pattern.
Since $\theta$ is a function of the minor vector, there are at most
$1+14580M^5\tau^*(M)\le14581(M+1)^5\Xi(a)$ patterns, by Propositions~\ref{prop:allminors}
and~\ref{prop:minorvec}. For the second bound, if $AT>28a$ then $T^4\le122449a$. Otherwise
$HAT\le56a$. If $M=0$ then
$T^4\le448\cdot24\cdot14581\,\Delta\Xi^2a$. Otherwise $Ta^{15}\le32\cdot24\cdot14581\cdot3609600^5
A^{20}H^{31}\Xi^2$, hence $T^{32}A^{11}\ll\Xi^2a^{16}$ and $T^{54}\le(8A\Delta)^{11}T^{32}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:827}]
Since $\Delta,\Xi(a)\ll_\eta a^\eta$, every window has $T\ll a^{(16+42\eta)/54}$, and $16/54=8/27$.
Choose $\eta$ sufficiently small in terms of $\varepsilon$, include the
bounded-length windows, and sum over the $O(\log a)$ dyadic windows.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
In terms of the scales, $T\ll a^\eta X^5H$, so $T^{32}\ll a^{5+\eta}B^{11}$ and $T^{54}\ll
a^{16+O(\eta)}$. At the critical point $T\approx a^{8/27}$, $B\approx a^{11/27}$, $H\approx a^{3/27}$
and $X\approx a^{1/27}$.
\end{remark}

\section{Lattices of minor vectors and the exponent \texorpdfstring{$45/152$}{45/152}}\label{sec:45152}

Proposition~\ref{prop:twostate3} allows $(2H+1)(\omega+4)$ gap vectors for every minor vector. But
the gap vector of a state is not an arbitrary point of $[1,H]^5$: it lies in a rank-three
lattice determined by its minor vector. If that lattice has no short vectors, the slices of the
ternary quadratic are few. If it has a short vector, the minor vector satisfies an extra
congruence, and few minor vectors do. Balancing the two proves Theorem~\ref{thm:45152}. The
argument uses only integer arithmetic. Instead of the geometry of numbers it uses a reduced
basis obtained by minimising determinants and lengths directly.

\subsection{The lattice of a minor vector}
Let $p\ne0$ be the minor vector of a $3\times5$ integer matrix $\mathcal M$, with pivot $\pi$,
$m=p_\pi$, pivot columns $c_1<c_2<c_3$, other columns $c_4<c_5$, and $u$, $v$ as in
Proposition~\ref{prop:minorvec}. Let $\Lambda_p\subseteq\mathbb Z^3$ be the subgroup of $x$ with
$m\mid u\cdot x$ and $m\mid v\cdot x$. It contains $m\mathbb Z^3$. Let $\lambda(p)$ be the least
sup-norm of a nonzero vector of $\Lambda_p$.
Subscripts on $L_c$ and $p_{abc}$ retain the original column labels
$0,\dots,4$; $c_1,c_2,c_3$ enumerate the chosen pivot. We use
$\gcd(n,x)$ for $\gcd(n,x_1,x_2,x_3)$ when $x\in\mathbb Z^3$.

\begin{lemma}[Lifting]\label{lem:lift}
Let $L_c$ be $me_a$ if $c=c_a$ is a pivot column, $u$ if $c=c_4$ and $v$ if $c=c_5$. For every
row $x$ of $\mathcal M$ and every column $c$, $m\,x_c=L_c\cdot(x_{c_1},x_{c_2},x_{c_3})$.
Moreover $\det(L_1,L_2,L_3)=m^2p_{123}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The rows $L_c$ are the rows of $\mathcal M^{\mathsf T}\operatorname{adj}(\mathcal M_\pi^{\mathsf
T})$, where $\mathcal M_\pi$ is the pivot $3\times3$ block. This is Cramer's rule. Both claims
are polynomial identities in the fifteen entries, checked symbolically for each of the ten
pivots.
\end{proof}

So the pivot gaps $k'=(k_{c_1},k_{c_2},k_{c_3})$ of a state lie in $\Lambda_p$. The five gaps
of the state are $k_c=L_c\cdot k'/m$, and since $p_{123}=L(i+1)\ge1$, the gaps $(k_1,k_2,k_3)$ are an
invertible linear image of $k'$.

\begin{lemma}[Index]\label{lem:index}
If $t$ is a nonnegative integer and $(t+1)^3>|m|$, then $\lambda(p)\le t$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $g=\gcd(|m|,u)$, $n=|m|/g$ and $u=gu_0$. The proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:minorvec}
gives $v=\mu u_0+nw$ with $\mu=c\cdot v\in\mathbb Z$ and $w\in\mathbb Z^3$. Put
$\psi(x)=\bigl(u_0\cdot x\bmod n,\ (\mu\lfloor u_0\cdot x/n\rfloor+w\cdot x)\bmod g\bigr)$,
which takes at most $ng=|m|$ values. Two points $x\ne y$ of $[0,t]^3$ with
$\psi(x)=\psi(y)$ exist. Then $u_0\cdot(x-y)=n(q-q')$ with $q,q'$ the two floors, so
$u\cdot(x-y)=\pm m(q-q')$, and $v\cdot(x-y)=n\bigl(\mu(q-q')+w\cdot(x-y)\bigr)$ with the
bracket divisible by $g$. So $x-y\in\Lambda_p$.
\end{proof}

Since $\psi(x)=\psi(y)$ forces $x-y\in\Lambda_p$, the same argument shows that $\Lambda_p$ has
index at most $|m|$ in $\mathbb Z^3$.

\subsection{Reduced bases}

Here $|v|$ denotes the Euclidean norm, while $|v|_\infty$ denotes the sup norm
used to define $\lambda(p)$. In particular, $|v|\ge |v|_\infty$.

\begin{lemma}[Reduction]\label{lem:reduce}
Let $\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb Z^3$ be a subgroup containing $m\mathbb Z^3$, $m\ge1$. There is a
basis $b_1,b_2,b_3$ of $\Lambda$ with $\delta=\det(b_1,b_2,b_3)$, $0<|\delta|\le m^3$ and
\[
27\,|b_1|^2|b_2|^2|b_3|^2\le64\,\delta^2 .
\]
Hence every $w\in\Lambda$ is $\sum y_ib_i$ with $y\in\mathbb Z^3$ and $27|b_i|^2y_i^2\le64|w|^2$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $I$ be the least nonzero $|\det|$ of a triple in $\Lambda$; the triple
$(me_1,me_2,me_3)$ gives $I\le m^3$. A triple with $|\det|=I$ is a basis. Otherwise some
$w\in\Lambda$ has a Cramer coordinate that is not an integer, say the first,
$\det(w,b_2,b_3)/\delta$, and $w-\lfloor\cdot\rfloor b_1$ gives a triple with smaller nonzero
determinant. Among such bases
minimise $d_1=|b_1|^2$, and then the Gram determinant $d_2=d_1|b_2|^2-\langle b_1,b_2\rangle^2$.
Put $d_3=\delta^2$.

Choose $n$ with $|2(\langle b_1,b_2\rangle+nd_1)|\le d_1$. Then $(b_2+nb_1,b_1,b_3)$ is a basis, so
$d_1\le|b_2+nb_1|^2$, and $d_1|b_2+nb_1|^2=d_2+(\langle b_1,b_2\rangle+nd_1)^2$. Hence
$3d_1^2\le4d_2$.

Let $B(y,z)=d_1\langle y,z\rangle-\langle b_1,y\rangle\langle b_1,z\rangle$, so that
$B(b_2,b_2)=d_2$, and Sylvester's identity gives $B(b_2,b_2)B(b_3,b_3)-B(b_2,b_3)^2=d_1d_3$. Choose
$n_2$ with $|2(B(b_2,b_3)+n_2d_2)|\le d_2$ and put $c=b_3+n_2b_2$. Then $(b_1,c,b_2)$ is a basis
with first vector $b_1$, so $d_2\le d_1|c|^2-\langle b_1,c\rangle^2=B(c,c)$, while
$d_2B(c,c)=(B(b_2,b_3)+n_2d_2)^2+d_1d_3$. Hence $3d_2^2\le4d_1d_3$.

Finally choose $n_1$ with $|2(\langle b_1,c\rangle+n_1d_1)|\le d_1$, and use the basis
$b_1$, $b_2'=b_2+nb_1$, $b_3'=c+n_1b_1$. The identities above give
$3d_1|b_2'|^2\le4d_2$ and $4d_1d_2|b_3'|^2\le d_2^2+4d_1d_3+d_1^2d_2$. With the two inequalities
this gives $9d_2|b_3'|^2\le16d_3$, and the product of the three bounds is the claim. For the
coordinates, $y_1\delta=\det(w,b_2,b_3)$ and Hadamard's inequality $\det(w,b_2,b_3)^2\le
|w|^2|b_2|^2|b_3|^2$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Few minor vectors have a short vector}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:dotcount}
For $n\ge1$ and $x\in\mathbb Z^3$, at most $27n^2\gcd(n,x)$ vectors $w\in[-n,n]^3$ have
$n\mid w\cdot x$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Reducing modulo $n$ loses a factor at most $27$. Let $K$ be the set of solutions in
$[0,n)^3$, $e=\gcd(n,x)$ and $c\cdot x\equiv e\pmod n$ by B\'ezout. The map
$(w,j)\mapsto(w+jc)\bmod n$ is injective on $K\times[0,n/e)$: equal images give
$n\mid(j-j')e$, so $j=j'$, and then $w=w'$. Hence $|K|\,n/e\le n^3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Short vectors]\label{prop:short}
Let $\mathcal S$ be a set of nonzero minor vectors with coordinates in $[-Y,Y]$, and let
$\tau(m)\le\tau_0$ for $m\le Y$. Then for every $R\ge1$,
\[
\#\{p\in\mathcal S:\lambda(p)\le R\}\le393660\,R^3Y^4\tau_0^2 .
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $\lambda(p)\le R$, some nonzero $x\in[-R,R]^3$ has $m\mid u\cdot x$. For fixed $x$, the
encoding of Proposition~\ref{prop:minorvec} gives at most $20\sum_m N(m,x)$ such $p$, where
$N(m,x)$ counts pairs $(u,v)$ with $m\mid u\times v$ and $m\mid u\cdot x$. For each $u$ there
are at most $27m\gcd(m,u)^2$ vectors $v$. Grouping the $u$ by $g=\gcd(m,u)$ and writing
$u=gu'$ with $(m/g)\mid u'\cdot x$, Lemma~\ref{lem:dotcount} gives
$\sum_ug^2\le27m^2\tau(m)\gcd(m,x)$, so $N(m,x)\le729m^3\tau(m)\gcd(m,x)$. Finally
$\sum_{0<|x|_\infty\le R}\gcd(m,x)\le27R^3\tau(m)$. A divisor $h>R$ of $m$ divides no nonzero
$x$ in the box, and for $h\le R$ the multiples of $h$ number at most $(2R/h+1)^3$, with
$h(2R/h+1)\le3R$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{One minor vector}

\begin{lemma}[Definite norm equations]\label{lem:defnorm}
For $D\ge1$ and $m\ne0$, the equation $x^2+Dy^2=m$ has at most $4\tau(|m|)^3$ integer
solutions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
As in Lemma~\ref{lem:norm}, with $D=df^2$, $d$ squarefree, each solution reduces to a primitive
$u^2+dw^2=n$, $m=g^2n$, with a class $t\equiv uw^{-1}$, a square root of $-d$ modulo $n$. The
count of square roots in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:norm} applies to $-d$ unchanged. Two
solutions in a class give integers $r,s$ with $r^2+ds^2=1$, of which there are at most four.
\end{proof}

A ternary quadratic $F$ with quadratic part of Gram matrix $S$ has $\det(2S)$ multiplied by
$\det(C)^2$ under a substitution $x=Cy$. In the notation of Lemma~\ref{lem:slice} one has the
identity $\ell=-2\alpha\det(2S)$. For $F_\theta$, Lemma~\ref{lem:tern} therefore gives
$\det(2S)=-8N_0N_1^2(N_0-u_2)(N_1+u_1)(u_1-u_2)\ne0$ under the hypotheses of Lemma~\ref{lem:signs},
so the following lemma applies to $F_\theta$ after any invertible linear change of variables.

\begin{lemma}[Plane substitution]\label{lem:planesub}
Let $F$ be a ternary quadratic with integer coefficients whose quadratic part has Gram matrix
$S$ with $\det(2S)\ne0$. There is a substitution $x=Cy$, where $C$ is a product of two integer
matrices with entries in $\{0,\pm1\}$, determinant $\pm1$ and inverses with absolute row sums
at most $2$, such that $F(Cy)$ has nonzero $y_1^2$ coefficient $\alpha$, nonzero binary
discriminant $D$ in $(y_1,y_2)$, and $\ell\ne0$, in the notation of Lemma~\ref{lem:slice}.
Moreover, the conclusion of Lemma~\ref{lem:slice} holds with $D>0$ replaced by $D\ne0$, under
the same hypothesis on $\omega$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $\ell=-2\alpha\det(2S)$ and $\det(2S)$ changes only by the factor $\det(C)^2=1$, it
suffices to make $\alpha$ and $D$ nonzero. First choose one of the planes
$\langle e_1,e_2\rangle$, $\langle e_1,e_3\rangle$, $\langle e_2,e_3\rangle$,
$\langle e_1+e_2,e_3\rangle$: if all four binary discriminants vanished then $\det(2S)$ would
vanish, by an explicit combination with polynomial coefficients. Writing $f,g$
for the displayed spanning vectors of the chosen plane, choose $f$, $g$ or
$f+g$ as its first basis vector. With this, Lemma~\ref{lem:slice} holds
for $\alpha\ne0$ and $D\ne0$ of either sign, under the same hypothesis on $\omega$. When
$D<0$ each slice uses Lemma~\ref{lem:defnorm}, whose bound $4\tau(|m|)^3$ is at most
$2\tau(|m|)^3(\log_3\bigl((11K^2H(3K+2))^2\bigr)+1)$ because $11K^2H(3K+2)\ge3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[One minor vector]\label{prop:fibre}
Let $p$ be a minor vector with all coordinates in $[-X,X]$, $X\ge1$, and suppose $\omega$ satisfies
the hypothesis of Lemma~\ref{lem:slice} for $K=248832X^8$ and $12H+1$ in place of $H$. The good
starts with minor vector $p$ have at most
\[
\bigl(8\lfloor3H/\lambda(p)\rfloor+3\bigr)(\omega+4)
\]
gap vectors $\kappa$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
At a good start the gaps lie in $[1,H]$, and since $N_0=L(i)$ and $N_1=L(i+1)$ are positive
integers, Lemma~\ref{lem:signs} gives $u_1>0>u_2$. Let $b_1,b_2,b_3$ be the basis of Lemma~\ref{lem:reduce} for $\Lambda_p$, with $|m|$ in
place of $m$, and $\hat b_i$ their lifts $c\mapsto L_c\cdot b_i/m$ to $\mathbb Z^5$. Write
$k'=\sum y_ib_i$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:lift}, $\kappa=\sum y_i\hat b_i$, so $(k_1,k_2,k_3)=\hat By$
for the $3\times3$ matrix $\hat B$ of the lifts in columns $1,2,3$, and
$m\det\hat B=p_{123}\delta\ne0$. So $y$ is a zero of $\tilde F=F_\theta\circ\hat B$, and
$\det(2S_{\tilde F})=\det(2S_{F_\theta})(\det\hat B)^2\ne0$. Each $|b_i|^2\ge\lambda(p)^2$ and
$|k'|^2\le3H^2$, so $\lambda(p)|y_i|\le3H$, and after the plane substitution the coordinates lie
in $[-Y',Y']$ with $Y'=4\lfloor3H/\lambda(p)\rfloor+1\le12H+1$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:planesub}).
Every nonzero integer basis vector has length at least one, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:reduce} gives $|b_i|\le2X^3$. Maximality of the pivot bounds
each entry of $L_c$ by $|m|$. Thus every lift is at most $6X^3$, and the coefficients of the final quadratic
are at most $12^3\cdot4X^2\cdot(6X^3)^2=248832X^8$. Lemma~\ref{lem:slice}, in the form of
Lemma~\ref{lem:planesub}, therefore bounds the zeros by $(2Y'+1)(\omega+4)$, and $y$ determines $\kappa$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The bound}

\begin{lemma}[Partial summation]\label{lem:abel}
Let $t$ and $R_p$ for $p\in\mathcal S$ be integers with $1\le R_p\le t$, and let
$\#\{p:R_p\le r\}\le Cr^3$ for $r\ge1$. Then
$t\sum_p\lfloor n/R_p\rfloor\le n|\mathcal S|+nCt^3$ for every $n\ge0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $1/r=1/t+\sum_{r\le j<t}1/(j(j+1))$ and exchange the sums:
$\sum_p1/R_p\le|\mathcal S|/t+\sum_{j<t}Cj^3/(j(j+1))\le|\mathcal S|/t+Ct^2$.
\end{proof}

Let $P_2(a)=10^{1000}a^{400}$ and $\Xi_2(a)=2\tau^*(P_2(a))^3(\log_3P_2(a)+1)+4$. As in
Section~\ref{sec:1137}, $\omega=\Xi_2(a)-4$ is admissible in every window, and
$\Xi_2(a)\ll_\eta a^\eta$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:twostate4}
For a window run in $[A,2A)$ with $T\ge36$, let $H=\lfloor28a/(AT)\rfloor+1$ and
$X=\lfloor3609600A^4H^6/a^3\rfloor+1$. Then
\[
T^3\ll\Xi_2(a)^9\,(H^3X^{14}+X^{15})\qquad\text{and}\qquad T^{152}\ll\Delta^{39}\Xi_2(a)^{117}a^{45}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Explicitly, we prove $T^3\le C_\star\,\Xi_2(a)^9\,(H^3X^{14}+X^{15})$ with $C_\star=32\cdot607461312^3$,
and $T^{152}\le C_{152}\Delta^{39}\Xi_2(a)^{117}a^{45}$ with $C_{152}=122449^{38}+c_0^{13}+8^{31}c_A+8^{30}c_B$, where
$c_0=1024\,C_\star\,56^3$, $c_A=2^{15}C_\star\,3609600^{14}56^{87}$ and $c_B=2^{16}C_\star\,3609600^{15}56^{90}$.
Let $\mathcal S$ be the set of minor vectors of good starts and $\tau_0=\tau^*(P_2(a))$. At most
four good starts share $(p,\kappa)$, and $T\le2|\mathcal G|$, so Proposition~\ref{prop:fibre}
gives
\[
T\le8\,\Xi_2(a)\Bigl(8\sum_{p\in\mathcal S}\lfloor3H/\lambda(p)\rfloor+3|\mathcal S|\Bigr).
\]
Choose $t$ with $X\le t^3\le8X$. Lemma~\ref{lem:index} gives $\lambda(p)\le t$, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:short} and Lemma~\ref{lem:abel} give
$t\sum_p\lfloor3H/\lambda(p)\rfloor\le3H|\mathcal S|+3H\cdot393660X^4\tau_0^2t^3$, with
$|\mathcal S|\le14581X^5\tau_0$. Hence $Tt\le607461312\,\Xi_2^3X^5(H+t)$, and cubing, with
$(H+t)^3\le4(H^3+t^3)$ and $X\le t^3\le8X$, gives the first bound.

For the second, if $AT>28a$ then $T^4\le122449a$. Otherwise $H\,AT\le56a$. If $X=1$ then
$T^6A^3\le2C_\star\,56^3\Xi_2^9a^3$, and $T^{12}\le(8A\Delta)^3T^6\le c_0\Delta^3\Xi_2^9a^3$. If
$X\ge2$, write $X=X_0+1\le2X_0$ with $X_0a^3\le3609600A^4H^6$. When $2X_0\le H^3$,
$T^3a^{42}\ll\Xi_2^9A^{56}H^{87}$, so $T^{90}A^{31}\le c_A\Xi_2^9a^{45}$ and
$T^{152}\le(8A\Delta)^{31}T^{90}$. When $2X_0>H^3$, $T^3a^{45}\ll\Xi_2^9A^{60}H^{90}$, so
$T^{93}A^{30}\le c_B\Xi_2^9a^{45}$ and $T^{153}\le(8A\Delta)^{30}T^{93}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:45152}]
Since $\Delta,\Xi_2(a)\ll_\eta a^\eta$, every window has $T\ll a^{(45+156\eta)/152}$. Summing over
the $O(\log a)$ windows proves the theorem.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
In terms of the scales, $T\ll a^\eta(HX^{14/3}+X^5)$: the factor $X^{1/3}$ saved over
Proposition~\ref{prop:twostate3} is the typical $\lambda(p)$. At the critical point
$T\approx a^{45/152}$, $B\approx a^{62/152}$, $H\approx a^{17/152}$ and $X\approx a^{6/152}$, and
the term $X^5\approx a^{30/152}$ is smaller.
\end{remark}

\section{Joint integer invariants and the optimized bound}\label{sec:joint}

We now prove Theorem~\ref{thm:joint}. Fix a window run in $[A,2A)$ with $T\ge36$
terms. Throughout this section constants may depend on fixed positive integers
$m,n$ (the powers of Section~\ref{sec:jointcount}) and on a loss $\eta>0$, but
never on the dividend, the orbit or the pattern. The argument has six steps.
\begin{enumerate}
\item For a fixed pattern, the nodes of every good start lie on at most
$24$ curves (Section~\ref{sec:jointcurve}).
\item On these curves the quotient $q_i$ and the curvature $I_3(i)$ are
rational functions of the nodes. Components on which the reconstruction
identities fail contribute $O(1)$ starts (Section~\ref{sec:jointrecon}).
\item The directions at infinity of the curves are controlled by the
Newton coefficients of the pattern. This makes nonexceptional
components have even degree (Section~\ref{sec:jointinfinity}), shows
that exceptional patterns are few, and excludes lines
(Section~\ref{sec:jointexc}).
\item The reconstructed functions, and hence two families of new integer
coordinates $W$ and $J$, have prescribed orders at infinity
(Sections~\ref{sec:jointorders} and~\ref{sec:jointcoords}).
\item Each nonexceptional conic contributes $a^{o(1)}$ starts
(Section~\ref{sec:jointconic}).
\item On the remaining components, adjoining $W_j^m$ and $J^n$ raises the
degree. The curve-counting theorem and a balancing of heights then give the
exponent (Sections~\ref{sec:jointgraph} and~\ref{sec:jointcount}).
\end{enumerate}

\emph{Notation.} The symbols $D$, $L$ and $W$ of earlier sections do not appear here
with their earlier meanings. In this section
\begin{itemize}
\item $\theta=(N_0,N_1,N_2,u_1,u_2,v_1,v_2)$ are the pattern integers of
Lemma~\ref{lem:quad}, with $N_0=I_4(i)$, and bars denote their normalised
versions~\eqref{eq:jointbar};
\item $s_j$ are the nodes, $k_j$ the gaps, $P_4,R_4$ the two orthogonal
quartics, $\Psi$ the quartic norm and $f$ the pattern quadratic;
\item $Q$ and $E_0$ are the reconstructed quotient and curvature,
$W=(W_0,W_1,W_2)$ and $J$ the joint coordinates, and $S$, $Y$ their height
bounds;
\item $e$ is a multiplicity at infinity, $d$ the degree of a component,
$\delta$ the degree of a graph and $\delta_{\min}=10n+7m$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Good starts and the pattern curve}\label{sec:jointcurve}

Put
\[
 H=\left\lfloor\frac{28a}{AT}\right\rfloor+1,\qquad
 X=\left\lfloor\frac{3609600 A^4H^6}{a^3}\right\rfloor.
\]
As in Proposition~\ref{prop:twostate}, call a start $i$ \emph{good} if the
window contains the nine states $i,\dots,i+8$ and the gaps $k_0,\dots,k_6$ at
state $i$ are at most $H$; at least $T/2$ starts are good. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:allminors}, the minor vector of a good start has all
coordinates at most $X$, and $X\ge1$ because its coordinate $p_{012}=L(i)$ is a
positive integer. We call this minor vector the \emph{pattern} of the start; it
determines $\theta(i)$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:minorvec}, there are
$O_\eta(a^\eta X^5)$ patterns.

The \emph{nodes} of state $i$ are $s_j=q_{i+j}-q_i$. They are the support
points of the measure $\mu_i$ of Section~\ref{sec:hankel}, and the
interpolation nodes of the Newton polynomials of Section~\ref{sec:2377}. The
three \emph{free nodes} $s_1,s_2,s_3$ of a good start are integers in $[0,3H]$.
Distinct good starts with the same pattern have distinct free-node triples
(Lemma~\ref{lem:jointcuts} below).

For the pattern $\theta$, put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jointbar}
 (\bar N_1,\bar u_1,\bar u_2)=\frac{(N_1,u_1,u_2)}{N_0},\qquad
 (\bar N_2,\bar v_1,\bar v_2)=\frac{(N_2,v_1,v_2)}{N_1}.
\end{equation}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:signs}, $\bar N_1>0$, $\bar u_1>0$ and $\bar u_2<0$.
Treat the free nodes $s_1,s_2,s_3$ as coordinates and define
\[
 s_0=0,\quad
 s_4=(\bar N_1+\bar u_1)s_1-(\bar u_1-\bar u_2)s_2-(\bar u_2-1)s_3,\quad
 k_j=s_{j+1}-s_j\quad(0\le j\le3),
\]
\[
 k_4=\bar N_2k_1-\bar v_1k_2-\bar v_2k_3,\qquad s_5=s_4+k_4.
\]
At a good start these are the actual nodes and gaps, by the two
$k$-relations of Lemma~\ref{lem:pattern}; the first is equivalent to
$\bar N_1k_0=\bar u_1k_1+\bar u_2k_2+k_3$. Let
\[
 \gamma=(\bar N_1,\,-\bar N_1-\bar u_1,\,\bar u_1-\bar u_2,\,\bar u_2-1,\,1),
\]
and let $\gamma'$ be the same expression in $\bar N_2,\bar v_1,\bar v_2$. Put
\[
 P_4(x)=\sum_{j=0}^4\gamma_j\prod_{\ell=0}^{j-1}(x-s_\ell),
 \qquad
 R_4(x)=\sum_{j=0}^4\gamma'_j\prod_{\ell=1}^{j}(x-s_\ell).
\]
By the remark after Lemma~\ref{lem:pattern}, at a good start $P_4$ is the
monic orthogonal polynomial $\pi_i$ of degree four for $\nu_i$, and
$R_4(x)=\pi_{i+1}(x-k_0)$ is the corresponding polynomial of the next state,
written in the variable of the current state. Put $Z=xR_4$ and
$f=\sum_{j=0}^4\gamma_j(s_j^2+k_j)$, the \emph{pattern quadratic}, and let
\begin{align*}
 \Psi={}&\sum_{j=0}^4\gamma_j
 \bigl(s_j^3+3s_j^2+3s_j(1+k_j)+(1+k_j)^2\bigr)
 +\sum_{j=0}^3\gamma_jk_{j+1}\\
 &+\bar N_2r_1-\bar v_1r_2-\bar v_2r_3-k_4(2s_4+k_4),
\end{align*}
where $r_j=k_j(s_j+s_{j+1})+k_{j+1}$ for $j=1,2,3$. This is a cubic
polynomial in the free nodes, the \emph{quartic norm}.

\begin{lemma}[The pattern curve]\label{lem:jointcurve}
\begin{enumerate}
\item At a good start $i$, $\Psi=\nu_i(P_4^2)>0$, and the free nodes satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jointcurve}
 f=0,\qquad N_0\operatorname{Res}(P_4,Z)=a\Psi^4.
\end{equation}
\item For every pattern with $\bar N_1,\bar u_1>0>\bar u_2$, the two
equations~\eqref{eq:jointcurve} have degrees two and at most twelve and no
common factor. Their common zero set in $\mathbb A^3$ is the union of at most
twenty-four integral affine curves over $\mathbb Q$, of total degree at most
twenty-four.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(1) Since $r_m=s_{m+1}^2-s_m^2+k_{m+1}$, summation by parts and the
$k$-relation at state $i$ show that $N_0f$ is the $r$-relation
$\sum_mU_m(i)r_m=0$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:pattern}. Next, the shift rule
$\mu_i\bigl(g\prod_{\ell<j}(x-s_\ell)\bigr)=\mu_{i+j}\bigl(g(x+s_j)\bigr)$ and
the moments listed in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:labels} give
$\mu_i(x^3P_4)=\sum_j\gamma_j\bigl(s_j^3+3s_j^2+3s_j(1+k_j)+(1+k_j)^2+k_{j+1}\bigr)$.
Eliminating $k_5$ with the $r$-relation at state $i+1$ turns this into $\Psi$.
Since $\nu_i(xg)=\mu_i(g)-q_i\nu_i(g)$ and $P_4$ is orthogonal to $x^3$,
$\nu_i(P_4^2)=\nu_i(x^4P_4)=\mu_i(x^3P_4)=\Psi$, and this is positive as in
the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:hankel}.

For the resultant, Lemma~\ref{lem:moments}(3) gives
$\nu_i\bigl(xg(x)\bigr)=\nu_{i+1}\bigl(g(x+k_0)\bigr)$. Hence, for $j<4$,
$\nu_i(x^jZ)=\nu_{i+1}\bigl((x+k_0)^j\pi_{i+1}(x)\bigr)=0$. So the monic
quintic $Z$ differs from the monic orthogonal polynomial $\pi^{(5)}$ of degree
five for $\nu_i$ by a multiple of $P_4$, and $\operatorname{Res}(P_4,Z)=
\operatorname{Res}(P_4,\pi^{(5)})$. Let $n_j$ be the squared $\nu_i$-norm of
the monic orthogonal polynomial of degree $j$. The three-term recurrence,
whose coefficients are $\beta_j=n_j/n_{j-1}$, gives
\[
 \operatorname{Res}(P_4,\pi^{(5)})=\beta_1\beta_2^2\beta_3^3\beta_4^4
 =\frac{n_4^4}{n_0n_1n_2n_3}
 =\frac{\Psi^4}{\det\bigl(\nu_i(x^{r+s})\bigr)_{r,s<4}}
 =\frac{a\Psi^4}{I_4(i)}.
\]
Since $N_0=I_4(i)$, this is the second equation.

(2) The degree bounds are immediate, since the resultant has total degree at
most ten in the nodes. The quadratic part of $f$ is irreducible, with nonzero
Hessian determinant. The degree-twelve part of the second equation is
$-a\bigl(\sum_j\gamma_js_j^3\bigr)^4$. If $f$ divided it, the quadratic part
of $f$ would divide $\sum_j\gamma_js_j^3$, and an explicit computation
excludes this. So the two equations have no common factor, their common
zero set is a curve, and by B\'ezout's theorem its components over
$\mathbb Q$ have total degree at most $2\cdot12$.
\end{proof}

Degrees throughout this section mean the leading coefficient of the eventual
affine Hilbert function, equivalently the degree of the projective closure.
``Integral'' means that the defining ideal is prime over $\mathbb Q$; it does
not assert geometric irreducibility or a rational parametrization.

\subsection{Reconstruction and retained components}\label{sec:jointrecon}

Two further identities distinguish the components on which the later rational
functions have the required behavior. We give their definitions before
stating the component bounds.

Form the $8\times9$ matrix of coefficients of
$P_4,xP_4,x^2P_4,x^3P_4,Z,xZ,x^2Z,x^3Z$ in the basis
$1,x,\dots,x^8$. Let $c_j$ be $(-1)^j$ times the minor obtained by
deleting column $j$, and define
\[
 Q=\frac{c_1-c_2}{c_1-c_0},\qquad
 E_0=a\,\frac{\det(c_{r+s})_{0\le r,s<3}(c_1-c_0)^3}
                  {(c_1^2-c_0c_2)^3}.
\]
At a good start the matrix annihilates the moment vector
$(h_i(0),\dots,h_i(8))$ and has rank eight. Indeed, the resultant in
\eqref{eq:jointcurve} is positive, so $P_4$ and $Z$ are coprime.
The identities
$q_ih_i(0)+h_i(1)=q_ih_i(1)+h_i(2)=1$, together with
$h_i(0)h_i(2)>h_i(1)^2$, then give
\[
 h_i(j)=\frac{c_j(c_1-c_0)}{c_1^2-c_0c_2}\quad(0\le j\le8),\qquad
 Q=q_i,\qquad E_0=I_3(i).
\]
In particular, all displayed denominators are nonzero at good starts.

To state the identities as equations on the curve, use the raw-moment
polynomials
\[
 M_0=1,\qquad
 M_{r+1}(k_0,k_1,\dots)
 =\sum_{t=0}^{r}\binom rt k_0^{r-t}M_t(k_1,k_2,\dots).
\]
Only $M_0,\dots,M_6$ are needed; they involve $k_0,\dots,k_4$.
At good starts, $M_r=\mu_i(x^r)$ for $0\le r\le6$, by the
weight recursion in Section~\ref{sec:hankel}. Set
\[
 H_3=\det(M_{r+s})_{0\le r,s<3},\qquad
 H_4=\det(M_{r+s})_{0\le r,s<4},\qquad
 H_{013}=\det(M_{r+s})_{\substack{r=0,1,2\\s=0,1,3}}.
\]
This $H_4$ is the polynomial of Lemma~\ref{lem:multid}. With
$p_3=\bar u_2-1-s_1-s_2-s_3$, the coefficient of $x^3$ in $P_4$, the
normalization and raw-resolvent identities are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jointcuts}
 N_0P_4(-Q)=aH_4,\qquad
 E_0P_4(-Q)=a\bigl((Q-p_3)H_3-H_{013}\bigr).
\end{equation}
Among components containing a good start, we call a component
\emph{retained} if both equations, after clearing denominators, hold
identically on it.

\begin{lemma}[Removing components by polynomial equations]\label{lem:jointcuts}
The two identities in \eqref{eq:jointcuts}, after clearing denominators,
have degrees at most $166$ and $404$, respectively. On a degree-$d$
component, either an identity holds everywhere or it holds at
$166d$ or $404d$ good starts at most, respectively. Consequently each
component that is not retained contributes $O(1)$ good starts.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $Q=n_Q/d_Q$ and $E_0=n_E/d_E$ using the cofactor expressions above.
Each cofactor has degree at most $40$, so
\[
 \deg n_Q,\deg d_Q\le40,\qquad
 \deg n_E,\deg d_E\le240.
\]
The latter bound follows from the $3\times3$ determinant and the cube
in the numerator, and the cube of a quadratic cofactor expression in the
denominator. The polynomial
$\widehat P=d_Q^4P_4(-n_Q/d_Q)$ has degree at most $163$: in Newton
form its longest product is
$n_Q\prod_{j=1}^3(n_Q+s_jd_Q)$, of degree at most $40+3\cdot41$,
because $s_0=0$. The cleared equations are
\begin{align*}
 \Phi_N&=N_0\widehat P-aH_4d_Q^4,\\
 \Phi_R&=n_E\widehat P
 -a\bigl((n_Q-p_3d_Q)H_3-H_{013}d_Q\bigr)d_Q^3d_E.
\end{align*}
Here $\deg H_3\le3$, $\deg H_4\le6$ and $\deg H_{013}\le4$.
Thus $\deg\Phi_N\le166$ and $\deg\Phi_R\le404$.

If a polynomial of degree $r$ does not vanish identically on an integral
curve of degree $d$, its zero set on that curve has at most $rd$ distinct
rational points, by the polynomial-section form of B\'ezout's bound.
Every good start satisfies both cleared equations. Moreover, the
free nodes and pattern recover $q_i=Q$, and $q_i$ strictly increases along
the fixed orbit. Thus distinct good starts of this pattern give distinct free-node triples.
Applying the point bound to whichever equation is not an identity proves
the claim. The absolute bound $d\le24$ makes the last estimate uniform.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Directions at infinity}\label{sec:jointinfinity}

Call a pattern \emph{exceptional} if two of the five Newton coefficients
$\gamma_j$ sum to zero. Since $\bar N_1>0$, $\bar u_1>0$ and $\bar u_2<0$,
every $\gamma_j$ is nonzero, and a pair sum can vanish only in one of the
four cases
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jointexceptional}
 \bar N_1+\bar u_2=1,\qquad \bar N_1+\bar u_2=0,\qquad \bar u_1=1,\qquad \bar N_1+\bar u_1=1.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the integer pattern coordinates satisfy
$N_1+u_2=N_0$, $N_1+u_2=0$, $u_1=N_0$ or $N_1+u_1=N_0$.

\begin{lemma}[Leading nodes and determinant orders]\label{lem:jointleading}
Let $C$ be an integral component of positive degree of
\eqref{eq:jointcurve}. Let $P$ be a closed point above infinity on the
normalization of the projective closure of $C$, write
$\operatorname{ord}=\operatorname{ord}_P$, and let $e>0$ be the
multiplicity of the hyperplane at infinity at $P$.
For a local parameter $\varpi$ at $P$, let $z_j$ be the residue of
$\varpi^es_j$, $0\le j\le4$.
Then the $z_j$ are not all equal and satisfy
\[
 \sum_{j=0}^4\gamma_jz_j^r=0\qquad(0\le r\le3).
\]
If two of these five leading nodes coincide, the pattern is exceptional.
For a nonexceptional pattern, therefore,
\begin{align*}
 \operatorname{ord}(s_l-s_j)&=-e\quad(0\le j<l\le4),\\
 \operatorname{ord}\operatorname{Res}(P_4,Z)&=-10e,\\
 \operatorname{ord}H_3&=-3e,\qquad \operatorname{ord}H_4=-6e.
\end{align*}
Positive order denotes a zero and negative order a pole.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
All nonzero rational constants have order zero. The multiplicity $e$ can
equivalently be written as
\[
 e=\max_{1\le j\le3}\{-\operatorname{ord}s_j\}>0.
\]
Thus a polynomial of total node degree at most $r$ has order at least
$-re$. The residues $z_j$ lie in the residue field of $P$; we do not
require $P$ to be rational.
We have $z_0=0$, and at least one of $z_1,z_2,z_3$ is nonzero.

\emph{The leading nodes are distinct.}
The definitions of the nodes give the moment equations of orders zero
and one. The highest-degree part of $f=0$ gives the equation of order two.
Finally, the highest-degree part of
$N_0\operatorname{Res}(P_4,Z)=a\Psi^4$ gives the equation of order three:
the resultant has total degree at most ten, whereas the cubic part of
$\Psi$ is $\sum_j\gamma_js_j^3$. Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:joint-leading-moments}
 \sum_{j=0}^4\gamma_jz_j^r=0\qquad(0\le r\le3).
\end{equation}
For the last of these, multiply the fixed-dividend equation by
$\varpi^{12e}$ and take residues; its left side vanishes and its right side
is $a(\sum_j\gamma_jz_j^3)^4$.

If two nodes coincided, there would be at most four distinct values among
the $z_j$. Grouping the sums in \eqref{eq:joint-leading-moments} by those
values and using the corresponding Vandermonde matrix shows that the
sum of the $\gamma_j$ in each group is zero.
Since there are five indices and at least two groups, some group has at
most two elements. It cannot have one, because every $\gamma_j$ is nonzero;
it must therefore give a vanishing pair sum. This proves the collision
assertion. For a nonexceptional pattern it is impossible, so all five
nodes are distinct, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:joint-node-orders}
 \operatorname{ord}(s_l-s_j)=-e\qquad(0\le j<l\le4).
\end{equation}

\emph{The determinant orders.}
Write $\mathcal R=\operatorname{Res}(P_4,Z)$.
The leading homogeneous parts needed below are
\begin{align}
 [\mathcal R]_{10}
   &=\gamma_0\gamma_1\gamma_2\gamma_3
        \prod_{0\le j<l\le4}(s_l-s_j), \label{eq:joint-resultant-leading}\\
 [H_3]_3&=k_0k_1(k_0+k_1),\notag\\
 [H_4]_6&=k_0k_1k_2(k_0+k_1)(k_1+k_2)(k_0+k_1+k_2).
 \label{eq:joint-hankel-leading}
\end{align}
Here $[F]_r$ denotes the homogeneous part of total node degree $r$.
For completeness, the degree-ten formula can be checked without expanding
the resultant. In the Newton basis beginning at $s_1,s_2,s_3$, its
$4\times4$ Bezout matrix for $P_4,R_4$ has entry degrees at most
$4-\max(i,j)$, for $0\le i,j<4$. Only the diagonal term of its determinant
can have degree ten. Its four leading diagonal entries are
\[
\begin{split}
 &-\gamma_1s_1(s_1-s_2)(s_1-s_3)(s_1-s_4),\\
 &-\gamma_2s_2(s_2-s_3)(s_2-s_4),\\
 &-\gamma_3s_3(s_3-s_4),\qquad -s_4.
\end{split}
\]
Their product gives the stated Vandermonde; the additional factor
$x$ in the second polynomial contributes
$P_4(0)=\gamma_0$.
The raw-moment recurrence gives
\[
 H_3=k_0k_1(k_0+k_1)+k_0k_2-k_1^2,
\]
and gives the displayed degree-six part of $H_4$, with remainder of
degree at most five. These are finite polynomial identities.

Every factor in these leading products has order $-e$ by
\eqref{eq:joint-node-orders}. The lower-degree terms have strictly larger
order, so they cannot cancel the leading terms. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:joint-determinant-orders}
 \operatorname{ord}\mathcal R=-10e,\qquad
 \operatorname{ord}H_3=-3e,\qquad
 \operatorname{ord}H_4=-6e.
\end{equation}
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Even component degrees]\label{cor:jointparity}
Every positive-degree nonexceptional component of \eqref{eq:jointcurve}
has even degree. At each point above infinity its hyperplane multiplicity
$e$ is even, and
$\operatorname{ord}(N_0\Psi)=-5e/2$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Taking orders in the fixed-dividend equation and using
Lemma~\ref{lem:jointleading} gives
$4\operatorname{ord}\Psi=-10e$.
Since orders are integers and $N_0\ne0$, this proves both local assertions.
The degree of the curve is the sum of the multiplicities $e$, weighted
by the residue-field degrees, so it is even. The retained identities
are not needed here.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exceptional patterns and line components}\label{sec:jointexc}

Exceptional patterns may have components of odd degree, including lines, but
there are fewer of them.

\begin{lemma}[Exceptional patterns and lines]\label{lem:jointcomponents}
There are $O(X^4\log(2X))=O_\eta(a^\eta X^4)$ exceptional patterns. In the range
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jointscale}
 124928 A^4H^5X<a^3,
\end{equation}
no line component contains a good start.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Each relation in \eqref{eq:jointexceptional} becomes a vanishing maximal
minor after a fixed invertible integer column change. The transformed
minor vector still has height $O(X)$. For example, if the original columns
are $C_0,\dots,C_4$, then
\[
 \det(C_1,C_2+C_0,C_3-C_0)=p_{123}-p_{013}-p_{012}
                              =N_1+u_2-N_0.
\]
The other three relations follow by the same column additions and
permutations.

A fixed zero minor reduces the Pl\"ucker relations to
three integer vectors $\mathbf x,\mathbf y,\mathbf z$ of height $O(X)$
with $\mathbf x\times\mathbf y=0$ and
$\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf z=\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf z=0$.
These vectors are signed rearrangements of the nine remaining minors,
so they determine the minor vector.
The case $\mathbf x=\mathbf y=0$ has $O(X^3)$ choices. Otherwise write
$\mathbf x=r\mathbf v$, $\mathbf y=s\mathbf v$ with $\mathbf v$ primitive.
In a dyadic range $R\le|\mathbf v|_\infty<2R$, there are $O(R^3)$
choices for $\mathbf v$ and $O((X/R)^2)$ for $(r,s)$.
The equation $\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf z=0$ has $O(X^2/R)$ solutions
in the box. Indeed, take a coordinate of absolute value
$n=|\mathbf v|_\infty$. Primitivity gives $n$ admissible pairs of the
other two coordinates modulo $n$, and the chosen coordinate is then
determined. Since $R\le n\ll X$, this gives $O(X^2/n)$ choices.
Each range therefore contributes $O(X^4)$, and summing over $O(\log(2X))$
ranges proves the pattern count. The crude bound $X\le10^{16}a^7$
absorbs its logarithm into $a^\eta$.

For a contained line, the degree-twelve part of \eqref{eq:jointcurve}
first forces the cubic coefficient of $\Psi$ to vanish. Then $\Psi^4$ has
degree at most eight, so the degree-ten resultant coefficient also
vanishes. By the leading forms and collision argument in
Lemma~\ref{lem:jointleading}, its direction satisfies one of the four
exceptional relations. The grouping argument there gives exactly two
groups of equal leading nodes, of sizes two and three. After scaling,
the five-node directions are, in the order of \eqref{eq:jointexceptional},
\[
 (0,1,1,0,1),\quad (0,1,1,0,0),\quad
 (0,0,1,1,0),\quad (0,1,0,0,1).
\]
Substitution of these directions into the resultant gives a polynomial
of degree at most six. Equation~\eqref{eq:jointcurve} then
forces $\Psi$ to have degree at most one along the line. Its quadratic
coefficient, together with $f=0$, gives respectively
\[
 s_3=0,\qquad k_1=1,\qquad k_2=1,\qquad s_4-s_1=\bar u_1-\bar u_2.
\]
The first is impossible at a good start. The polynomial $G$ in
Lemma~\ref{lem:labels} satisfies, by expansion,
\[
 G\le H^5(12k_1+464),\qquad G\le H^5(12k_2+464).
\]
In the last obstruction, $k_1+k_2+k_3=\bar u_1-\bar u_2=(u_1-u_2)/N_0\le2X$.
Thus each surviving case gives $k_1\le2X$ or $k_2\le2X$.
The label bound $N_0q_i^4\le aG$, with $N_0\ge1$, now gives
\[
 q_i^4\le aH^5(24X+464).
\]
But $q_i\ge a/(4A)$ and $24X+464\le488X$ for $X\ge1$.
Condition~\eqref{eq:jointscale}, with $124928=4^4\cdot488$, gives
the strict reverse inequality.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Orders of the reconstructed functions}\label{sec:jointorders}

\begin{lemma}[Orders of the reconstructed functions]\label{lem:joint-infinity-orders}
Let $C$ be a retained nonexceptional component of positive degree
containing a good start.
At every point above infinity, with hyperplane multiplicity $e$, one has
\[
 \operatorname{ord}Q=-\frac{3e}{2},\qquad
 \operatorname{ord}E_0=\frac{3e}{2}.
\]
In particular, $E_0$ is a nonzero rational function vanishing at every
point above infinity.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The good start guarantees that the reconstruction denominators are
not identically zero on $C$. Thus $Q$ and $E_0$ are rational functions
on $C$, and retention makes both identities \eqref{eq:jointcuts}
identities in its function field.

\emph{The quotient order.}
Write
$P_4(x)=x^4+p_3x^3+p_2x^2+p_1x+p_0$.
Its Newton expression gives $\deg p_j\le4-j$, and hence
$\operatorname{ord}p_j\ge-(4-j)e$.
The first retained identity gives
\[
 \operatorname{ord}P_4(-Q)=\operatorname{ord}(aH_4/N_0)=-6e.
\]
If $\operatorname{ord}Q\ge-e$, every term of $P_4(-Q)$ would have order
at least $-4e$, a contradiction. Put $r=\operatorname{ord}Q<-e$.
For $0\le j<4$,
\[
 \operatorname{ord}\bigl(p_j(-Q)^j\bigr)-4r
 \ \ge\ (4-j)(-e-r)>0.
\]
Thus $Q^4$ is the unique term of lowest order. It follows that
$4r=-6e$, or $\operatorname{ord}Q=-3e/2$.

\emph{The reconstructed curvature order.}
The coefficient $p_3=\bar u_2-1-s_1-s_2-s_3$ has order at least $-e$.
The raw-moment recurrence also shows that $H_{013}$ has degree at most
four: its degree-four part is
$(2k_0+k_1)k_0k_1(k_0+k_1)$.
Consequently
\[
 \operatorname{ord}(Q-p_3)=-3e/2,\qquad
 \operatorname{ord}\bigl((Q-p_3)H_3\bigr)=-9e/2
       <-4e\le\operatorname{ord}H_{013}.
\]
There is again a unique term of lowest order. Dividing the second
retained identity by $P_4(-Q)$ therefore gives
\[
 \operatorname{ord}E_0=-9e/2-(-6e)=3e/2.
\]
The argument uses the discrete valuation on the normalization, so it
also covers branches above singular points of the original curve.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The joint coordinates}\label{sec:jointcoords}

Put $d_j=b_{i+j}-b_{i+j+1}$ and
\[
 e_{01}=d_0k_1-d_1k_0,\quad e_{02}=d_0k_2-d_2k_0,\quad
 e_{12}=d_1k_2-d_2k_1.
\]
The first of these, $e_{01}$, is the curvature $I_3(i)$. With $s_j=q_{i+j}-q_i$ and
$r_j=k_j(s_j+s_{j+1})+k_{j+1}$, write
\begin{align*}
 B_0&=k_0r_1-k_1r_0=k_0^2k_1+(k_0-1)k_1^2+k_0k_2,\\
 B_1&=k_1r_2-k_2r_1=k_1^2k_2+(k_1-1)k_2^2+k_1k_3.
\end{align*}
They are positive at every state at which they are defined, and $B_0=H_3$. On a retained component,
use the reconstructed function $E_0$ to define
\[
 E_{12}=\frac{B_1E_0-N_0k_1}{B_0},\qquad
 E_{02}=\frac{k_0E_{12}+k_2E_0}{k_1},\qquad
 W=(E_{12},-E_{02},E_0),\qquad J=N_0\Psi.
\]
The determinant and cross-product identities give, identically,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jointdot}
 W\cdot(k_0,k_1,k_2)=0,\qquad W\cdot(r_0,r_1,r_2)=N_0.
\end{equation}
At a good start these rational functions take the integer values
$W=(e_{12},-e_{02},e_{01})$. Changing
the rank-five Gram basis to $1,x,x^2,x^3,P_4$ gives
$\det(\nu_i(x^{r+s}))_{r,s<5}=\det(\nu_i(x^{r+s}))_{r,s<4}\nu_i(P_4^2)$.
Consequently $J=I_5(i)$ is a positive integer at good starts. The heights of these
integers are controlled by
\[
 S=\left\lfloor\frac{4352 A^3H^3}{a^2}\right\rfloor,\qquad
 Y=\left\lfloor\frac{1259991040 A^5H^{10}}{a^4}\right\rfloor.
\]

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:jointheights}
At good starts, $|W_j|\le S$ and $1\le J\le Y$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $q=q_i$ and use the exact positive remainder expansion
$(q+y)^{-1}=q^{-1}-yq^{-2}+y^2/(q^2(q+y))$. For the first four shifted states,
\[
 \frac{b_{i+j}}a=\frac1q-\frac{s_j+1}{q^2}+\epsilon_j,
 \qquad 0\le\epsilon_j\le17H^2/q^3.
\]
Thus $d_j/a=k_j/q^2+O(34H^2/q^3)$, and cancellation of the leading terms in
each minor gives $|e_{jl}|q^3\le68aH^3$. The bound $q\ge a/(4A)$ gives $S$,
including the floor because the minors are integers.
For $J$, Newton basis Gram comparison bounds the raw rank-five determinant by
$(1\cdot2\cdot10\cdot77\cdot799)H^{10}=1230460H^{10}$.
Weighted Gram comparison gives $Jq^5\le1230460aH^{10}$; the same lower bound
for $q$ gives $Y$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Orders of the joint coordinates]\label{cor:joint-curvature-orders}
In the setting of Lemma~\ref{lem:joint-infinity-orders}, the joint
coordinates satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jointorders}
 \operatorname{ord}W_0=\operatorname{ord}W_2=3e/2,\qquad
 \operatorname{ord}W_1\ge3e/2,\qquad
 \operatorname{ord}J=-5e/2.
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The cubic leading part of $B_1$ is $k_1k_2(k_1+k_2)$.
By \eqref{eq:joint-node-orders}, it has order $-3e$, whereas the
quadratic remainder $k_1k_3-k_2^2$ has order at least $-2e$.
Thus $\operatorname{ord}B_0=\operatorname{ord}B_1=-3e$ and
$\operatorname{ord}k_1=-e$.
In the numerator defining $E_{12}$ the two terms have different orders:
\[
 \operatorname{ord}(B_1E_0)=-3e/2
       <-e=\operatorname{ord}(N_0k_1).
\]
Therefore $\operatorname{ord}E_{12}=3e/2$.
Each term in $k_0E_{12}+k_2E_0$ has order $e/2$, so their sum has
order at least $e/2$. Dividing by $k_1$ gives
$\operatorname{ord}E_{02}\ge3e/2$. Cancellation here is harmless:
equality for the first and third coordinates already gives
$\min_j\operatorname{ord}W_j=3e/2$.
The formula for $J=N_0\Psi$ is Corollary~\ref{cor:jointparity}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Counting the nonexceptional conics}\label{sec:jointconic}

We now show that a nonexceptional degree-two component contributes only
$O_\eta(a^\eta)$ good starts. The implied constant is independent of
the pattern and of the component. A component that is not retained
already contributes at most $404\cdot2=808$ starts, by
Lemma~\ref{lem:jointcuts}. We may therefore assume that the component
$C$ is retained. If it contains at most two free-node triples of good starts,
there is nothing to prove. In the remaining case we first construct a
parameter whose value is an integer at every such triple.

\begin{lemma}[A bounded integral parameter]\label{lem:jointconicparameter}
Let $C$ be a retained nonexceptional degree-two component containing the
free-node triples of at least three good starts. After permuting the three
free-node coordinates to $(x,y,z)$, there are
an integer $\Delta_c\ne0$ and polynomials $\xi_0,\xi_1,\xi_2\in\mathbb Z[t]$ such
that
\[
 \deg \xi_j\le2,\qquad
 |\Delta_c|,\ \max_{j,k}|[t^k]\xi_j|\le(aX)^{O(1)}.
\]
The map
\[
 t\longmapsto
 \bigl(\xi_0(t),\xi_1(t),\xi_2(t)\bigr)/\Delta_c
\]
parametrizes $C$ over $\mathbb Q$. Its inverse takes an integer value at
every integer point of $C$, and is injective on those points.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Every free node of a good start has absolute value at most $a$. Three distinct
such triples admit an integer plane
\[
 \lambda_zz+\lambda_xx+\lambda_yy+\lambda_1=0,\qquad
 |\lambda_z|,|\lambda_x|,|\lambda_y|\le8a^2,\qquad |\lambda_1|\le24a^3.
\]
For example, take a cross product of two differences to obtain the
normal; if that cross product vanishes, a nonzero perpendicular vector
with the same bounds suffices. Put $K_{\mathrm p}=24a^3$. A plane not containing an integral curve
of degree two meets it in at most two distinct points. Thus this plane
contains $C$. Choose the eliminated coordinate $z$ with $\lambda_z\ne0$.

The six rational pattern coordinates~\eqref{eq:jointbar} have common denominator
$N_0N_1\ne0$ and respective integer numerators
\[
 N_1^2,\ u_1N_1,\ u_2N_1,\ N_2N_0,\ v_1N_0,\ v_2N_0,
\]
each of absolute value at most $X^2$. Expanding the quadratic $f$, its
homogeneous part is a diagonal quadratic plus the square of a linear
form. Its three diagonal numerators have denominator $N_0N_1$, and
its three linear numerators have denominator $(N_0N_1)^2$; all six
numerators have absolute value at most $12X^4$.
Substitute $z=-(\lambda_xx+\lambda_yy+\lambda_1)/\lambda_z$ and multiply by
$(N_0N_1\lambda_z)^2$. The resulting plane equation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jointconicplane}
 \alpha x^2+\beta xy+\gamma_c y^2+d_cx+e_cy+f_c=0,
\end{equation}
with integer coefficients of absolute value at most
$K=10(12X^4)^2K_{\mathrm p}^2$. Indeed, each
coefficient is a sum of products of two quantities bounded by $12X^4$
and two plane coefficients bounded by $K_{\mathrm p}$; expansion gives the stated
factor $10$. The restriction of $f$ to the plane is nonzero: otherwise
the plane would be a component of the irreducible pattern quadric.
It has degree two because it contains $C$, and hence defines $C$ in
that plane.

The pullback of the hyperplane at infinity to the normalization of $C$
has degree two. Corollary~\ref{cor:jointparity} says
that each of its positive multiplicities is even. Counting residue
degrees, there is therefore just one branch over infinity, of
multiplicity two and residue degree one. Consequently the leading
binary quadratic in \eqref{eq:jointconicplane} is a nonzero rational
scalar times the square of a rational linear form. Indeed, the
normalization map is finite and surjective, so the projective conic
meets the line at infinity only at the image of this rational point.
Its leading binary quadratic therefore has a single rational zero,
of multiplicity two. Interchanging $x$ and $y$ if necessary gives
\[
 \alpha\ne0,\qquad \beta^2-4\alpha\gamma_c=0.
\]
Set
\[
 \kappa_c=2\beta d_c-4\alpha e_c,\qquad
 \epsilon_c=d_c^2-4\alpha f_c,\qquad
 t=2\alpha x+\beta y+d_c.
\]
Completing the square in \eqref{eq:jointconicplane} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jointconicparameter}
 t^2=\kappa_c y+\epsilon_c.
\end{equation}
Here $\kappa_c\ne0$. If it were zero, a rational point of $C$ with
parameter $t_0$ would give $\epsilon_c=t_0^2$, and the equation would
factor as $(t-t_0)(t+t_0)=0$. Integrality would then place $C$ in an
affine line, contradicting its degree.

We can therefore solve explicitly:
\[
 y=\frac{t^2-\epsilon_c}{\kappa_c},\qquad
 x=\frac{t-\beta y-d_c}{2\alpha},\qquad
 z=-\frac{\lambda_xx+\lambda_yy+\lambda_1}{\lambda_z}.
\]
Substitution verifies the conic and plane equations for every rational
$t$, and the displayed linear expression in $x,y$ recovers $t$.
In particular, $t$ is an integer whenever $(x,y,z)$ is an integer point.
Distinct points have distinct parameters.
Taking $\Delta_c=2\alpha\kappa_c\lambda_z$ clears all three denominators.
The inequalities
\[
 |\kappa_c|\le6K^2,\qquad |\epsilon_c|\le5K^2,\qquad
 |\beta\epsilon_c-d_c\kappa_c|\le11K^3
\]
give $|\Delta_c|\le12K_{\mathrm p}K^3$ and the coefficient bounds $33K_{\mathrm p}K^3$
for the three resulting quadratic numerators. For good starts one
also has $|t|\le4Ka$, although the counting argument below does not
need a parameter interval.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Properness and coefficient height]\label{lem:jointconicproper}
In the parameter of Lemma~\ref{lem:jointconicparameter}, write
\[
 E_0(t)=\frac{F_E(t)}{G_E(t)},\qquad F_E,G_E\in\mathbb Z[t],
 \qquad \gcd_{\mathbb Q[t]}(F_E,G_E)=1.
\]
The polynomials may be chosen so that
\[
 \deg G_E-\deg F_E=3,\qquad
 \max(\deg F_E,\deg G_E)\le D_0,\qquad
 \max_k\bigl(|[t^k]F_E|,|[t^k]G_E|\bigr)\le C_0a^{k_0},
\]
for absolute constants $D_0,C_0,k_0$. In particular, $G_E$ is
nonconstant. At the parameter $t$ of every good start, $G_E(t)\ne0$ and
$F_E(t)/G_E(t)=I_3(i)$ is an integer.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The explicit parametrization identifies $C$ with the affine line and
its smooth projective model with $\mathbb P^1$. Its point at infinity
has hyperplane multiplicity two. Lemma~\ref{lem:joint-infinity-orders} therefore gives
\[
 \operatorname{ord}_{t=\infty}E_0=3.
\]
The function is nonzero, since it is positive at a good start.
For a reduced rational function on $\mathbb P^1$, its order at infinity
is $\deg G_E-\deg F_E$, proving properness.

We next bound the coefficients uniformly in the component, including
after cancellation. Retain $K_{\mathrm p}=24a^3$ and
$K=10(12X^4)^2K_{\mathrm p}^2$ from the preceding proof, and define
\[
 K_{\mathrm h}=1+a+X+33K_{\mathrm p}K^3.
\]
The six rational pattern coordinates, the three coordinate polynomials
$\xi_j/\Delta_c$, and the fixed dividend $a$ have the common nonzero
denominator $N_0N_1\Delta_c$. Their ten numerator polynomials have degree
at most two and coefficient height at most $K_{\mathrm h}^4$; the common denominator
has absolute value at most $K_{\mathrm h}^4$ as well. For instance, the coordinate
numerators are $N_0N_1\xi_j$, while the numerator for $a$ is
$aN_0N_1\Delta_c$.

The cofactor formula defining $E_0$ is a quotient of two fixed
polynomials in these ten inputs, with fixed rational coefficients.
Clear those fixed coefficients once, then substitute the ten
polynomials and clear a fixed power of their common denominator.
This gives integer polynomials $\tilde n,\tilde d$, both nonzero, of bounded degree
and coefficient height at most $CK_{\mathrm h}^r$, where the degree bound, $C$
and $r$ are absolute. This assertion follows directly from polynomial
multiplication: both the number of factors and the number of summands
are fixed by the displayed cofactor formula.

Choose a primitive $\Pi\in\mathbb Z[t]$ representing
$\gcd_{\mathbb Q[t]}(\tilde n,\tilde d)$. By Gauss's lemma, $\Pi$ divides both $\tilde n$ and
$\tilde d$ in $\mathbb Z[t]$. Thus $F_E=\tilde n/\Pi$ and $G_E=\tilde d/\Pi$ are integer polynomials
with the same ratio, coprime over $\mathbb Q$, and with
degrees no larger than those of $\tilde n,\tilde d$. For a nonzero integer polynomial of
degree at most $D_0$ and coefficient height $\mathcal H$, every integer
polynomial factor has coefficient height at most
$2^{D_0}(D_0+1)\mathcal H$. To see this, the Mahler measure of an integer
cofactor is at least one, the original polynomial's Mahler measure is
at most $(D_0+1)\mathcal H$, and each coefficient of a factor is bounded by
$2^{D_0}$ times its Mahler measure. Thus cancellation preserves a
fixed polynomial bound in $K_{\mathrm h}$.

Finally, $K_{\mathrm p}=24a^3$ and $X\le10^{16}a^7$ show that $K_{\mathrm h}$ is bounded by a fixed power of $a$.
Absorbing the fixed exponents and constants gives the asserted
$C_0a^{k_0}$ bound. The original reconstruction denominator is nonzero
at every good start. Its cleared polynomial $\tilde d$ is therefore
nonzero at the corresponding parameter, and so is its factor $G_E$.
The reconstruction identity then gives $F_E(t)/G_E(t)=I_3(i)\in\mathbb Z$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Integer values of a rational function]\label{lem:jointconicdivisors}
Let $F_E,G_E\in\mathbb Z[t]$ be coprime over $\mathbb Q$, of degrees at most
$D_0$, with $G_E$ nonconstant and coefficient heights at most
$C_0a^{k_0}$. For every $\eta>0$, the number of integers $t$ such that
$G_E(t)\ne0$ and $F_E(t)/G_E(t)\in\mathbb Z$ is $O_\eta(a^\eta)$.
The implied constant depends only on $\eta,D_0,C_0,k_0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The integer resultant $R=\operatorname{Res}(F_E,G_E)$ is nonzero.
The adjugate of the Sylvester matrix gives $U,V\in\mathbb Z[t]$ with
$UF_E+VG_E=R$. At any parameter in question, $G_E(t)\mid F_E(t)$, and hence
$G_E(t)\mid R$. There are $2\tau(|R|)$ possible signed values of $G_E(t)$.
For each such value $v$, the nonzero polynomial $G_E-v$ has at most
$\deg G_E\le D_0$ roots. Thus the number of parameters is at most
\[
 2D_0\tau(|R|).
\]
The Sylvester matrix has size at most $2D_0$, and its entries are
bounded by $C_0a^{k_0}$. Expanding its determinant gives
\[
 1\le |R|\le(2D_0)!\bigl(C_0a^{k_0}\bigr)^{2D_0}.
\]
The divisor estimate $\tau(n)\ll_\varepsilon n^\varepsilon$, with
$\varepsilon$ chosen in terms of $\eta$ and the fixed degree and height
exponents, proves the claim.
\end{proof}

Apply the last lemma to the reconstructed invariant $E_0$. The
parameter is injective on free-node triples, and reconstruction
of the strictly increasing $q_i$ makes those triples injective in the
good starts. Each retained nonexceptional conic therefore
contributes $O_\eta(a^\eta)$ starts. The components with at most two
free-node triples of good starts and the nonretained components were already bounded
absolutely. Apply the conic estimate with $\eta/2$ and the pattern
estimate $O_{\eta/2}(a^{\eta/2}X^5)$. Since there are at most twenty-four
components per pattern, all nonexceptional conics together contribute
$O_\eta(a^\eta X^5)$.

\subsection{The extra degree and the powered graph}\label{sec:jointgraph}

The conic count leaves retained nonexceptional components of degree at
least four. Fix such a component $C$ of degree $d$. The additional
integer coordinates will increase its degree without increasing their
heights too much. The key is one extra zero over the affine part of $C$.

All divisors in this argument are on the normalization $\widetilde C$
of the projective closure of $C$, over $\mathbb Q$.
For a closed point $P$ of $\widetilde C$, put
$f_P=[\kappa(P):\mathbb Q]$, where $\kappa(P)$ is the residue field; thus
$\deg(\sum_P a_P[P])=\sum_P a_Pf_P$.
Let $E_\infty=\sum_{P\mid\infty}e_P[P]$ be the pullback of the original
hyperplane at infinity. Then
$\sum_{P\mid\infty}e_Pf_P=d$.  We do not require the closed points, or the
branches over a rational point of $C$, to be rational.

Let $\mathcal F$ be the pole envelope of the curvature coordinates:
\[
 \mathcal F=\sum_P \max\{0,-\operatorname{ord}_P(W_0),
                   -\operatorname{ord}_P(W_1),
                   -\operatorname{ord}_P(W_2)\}[P],
\]
with $\operatorname{ord}_P(0)=+\infty$.
The orders in \eqref{eq:jointorders} show that $\mathcal F$ is supported over the
affine part of $C$, and hence is disjoint from $E_\infty$.

\begin{lemma}[An additional affine zero]\label{lem:jointaffinezero}
There is a rational linear combination $\phi$ of $W_0,W_1,W_2$ which is
not the zero function and has a positive-order zero at a closed point
$P_0$ of $\widetilde C$ outside $E_\infty$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
First, the $W_j$ cannot all be scalar multiples, in $\mathbb Q(C)$, of a
fixed nonzero vector $v\in\mathbb Q^3$.  Such a relation, together with
$W\cdot(k_0,k_1,k_2)=0$, would put $C$ in the plane
$v_0k_0+v_1k_1+v_2k_2=0$.  This is a genuine plane: the first three gaps
are an invertible linear change of the three node coordinates.
The pattern quadratic is irreducible, so its restriction to this plane
is nonzero and has degree at most two.  Every curve contained in this
plane section therefore has degree at most two, contrary to $d\ge4$.

Choose the free-node triple $z_*\in C(\mathbb Q)$ of a good start.  All the $W_j$
are regular there, because their common reconstruction denominator
does not vanish at a good start.  Write $w_j=W_j(z_*)$.
The identity $W\cdot(r_0,r_1,r_2)=N_0\ne0$ gives $w\ne0$.
Choose $h$ with $w_h\ne0$.  At least one function
$w_hW_j-w_jW_h$ is nonzero: otherwise
$W=(W_h/w_h)w$, contradicting the preceding paragraph.
Take such a function for $\phi$.  It has rational coefficients and
satisfies $\phi(z_*)=0$.

This zero survives normalization even if $z_*$ is singular.
Indeed, write $\phi=g/h_0$ near $z_*$, with $g$ not identically zero on $C$,
$g(z_*)=0$ and $h_0(z_*)\ne0$.
The finite normalization has a closed point $P_0$ above $z_*$.
In its discrete valuation ring the numerator is in the maximal ideal
and the denominator is a unit, so $\operatorname{ord}_{P_0}(\phi)>0$.
Since $z_*$ is affine, $P_0$ does not lie in $E_\infty$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The curvature pole degree]\label{lem:jointpoledegree}
The pole envelope satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jointextra}
 \deg \mathcal F\ge \frac{3d}{2}+1.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Use the function $\phi$ from Lemma~\ref{lem:jointaffinezero}.
At every point over infinity, each $W_j$ has order at least $3e_P/2$.
The same is true of a rational linear combination; cancellation can
only increase its zero order.  Consequently
\[
 (\phi)_0\ \ge\ \frac32 E_\infty+[P_0],
 \qquad
 (\phi)_\infty\ \le\ \mathcal F .
\]
The first divisor is integral because every $e_P$ is even.
The function $\phi$ is nonzero and has a zero, so its principal divisor
has equal zero and pole degrees.  Hence
\[
 \deg \mathcal F\ \ge\ \deg(\phi)_\infty
 =\deg(\phi)_0
 \ \ge\ \frac32\sum_{P\mid\infty}e_Pf_P+f_{P_0}
 \ \ge\ \frac{3d}{2}+1.
\]
The last inequality uses only $f_{P_0}\ge1$, not that $P_0$ is rational.
\end{proof}

For positive integers $m,n$, let $\Gamma_{m,n}\subseteq\mathbb A^7$ be
the closure of the graph obtained by adjoining
$W_0^m,W_1^m,W_2^m,J^n$ to the three original node coordinates.
The graph is taken on the common domain of these rational functions.
It is an integral curve, and projection to the first three coordinates
is birational: on that domain the four remaining coordinates are
uniquely determined.  Thus $\Gamma_{m,n}$ and $C$ have the same function
field.  Taking powers does not lose this property, because the original
coordinates remain in the graph.

\begin{lemma}[Degree of the powered graph]\label{lem:jointpowereddegree}
If $\delta=\deg\Gamma_{m,n}$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jointdegree}
 2\delta\ge(5n+3m)d+2m,
 \qquad
 \delta\ge10n+7m=:\delta_{\min}.
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\[
 \delta\le\bigl(247(m+n)+1\bigr)d.
\]
For $m=n=1$ one has the sharper upper bound $\delta\le247d$ and the
lower bound $\delta\ge4d+1\ge17$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Lower bound.}
At a point $P$ over the old infinity hyperplane, the original node
coordinates have pole orders at most $e_P$, while
$J^n$ has pole order $5ne_P/2$ and all $W_j^m$ vanish.
Since $n\ge1$, the pole envelope of the seven graph coordinates at
$P$ is exactly $5ne_P/2$.
Over the affine part, the node coordinates and $J$ are regular:
$J=N_0\Psi$ is polynomial in the nodes.  There the envelope is exactly
$m$ times the curvature envelope.  The complete envelope of the graph
coordinates is therefore
\[
 \mathcal F_{m,n}=\frac{5n}{2}E_\infty+m\mathcal F.
\]

There is a single rational linear combination $\lambda$ of the seven
coordinates whose pole divisor is $\mathcal F_{m,n}$.  To see this, fix a point $P$
in the finite support of $\mathcal F_{m,n}$ and multiply all seven coordinates by a
local uniformizer to the power $\mathcal F_{m,n}(P)$.  Their residues in $\kappa(P)$
are not all zero.  Cancellation of the leading pole is therefore a
proper $\mathbb Q$-linear condition on the seven coefficients.
The finitely many such proper subspaces do not cover $\mathbb Q^7$.
Choosing coefficients outside their union gives the asserted
simultaneous absence of cancellation, also when $\kappa(P)\ne\mathbb Q$.

As a linear function on $\Gamma_{m,n}$, $\lambda$ has degree at most
$\delta$.  For example, homogenize it on the projective closure of the
graph: its pencil with the homogenizing coordinate consists of
hyperplane sections of degree $\delta$.  Removing common zeros can
only decrease the degree of the resulting map to $\mathbb P^1$.
Birational projection identifies its function field with $\mathbb Q(C)$,
so this degree is $\deg(\lambda)_\infty=\deg \mathcal F_{m,n}$.  It follows that
\[
 \delta\ \ge\ \frac{5n}{2}d+m\deg \mathcal F
 \ \ge\ \frac{5n+3m}{2}d+m.
\]
This is the first inequality in \eqref{eq:jointdegree}; $d\ge4$ gives
the second.  The auxiliary linear combination is used only to measure
degree: no bound on its coefficients enters the integer-point box.

\emph{Upper bound.}
Here only total polynomial degrees matter, not the heights of the
pattern-dependent coefficients.
Write the cofactor formula for the reconstructed invariant as
$E_0=n_E/d_E$, as in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:jointcuts}.
Each entry of the moment matrix has degree at most four in the nodes.
Expanding its $8\times8$ cofactors gives, in particular, the uniform
bound $\deg c_j\le40$, so
$\deg n_E,\deg d_E\le240$.
Since $B_0,B_1$ have degree at most three and the gaps have degree at
most one, the three curvature coordinates have a common denominator
\[
 \Omega=d_E B_0k_1,\qquad \deg\Omega\le244,
\]
and numerators $\mathcal N_0,\mathcal N_1,\mathcal N_2$ of degree at
most $244$.  The numerator for $J$ with this denominator is
$\mathcal N_3=J\Omega$, of degree at most $247$.
The denominator is nonzero on $C$, since it is nonzero at a good start.

Put $\bar m=m+n$.  Represent the powered coordinates with denominator
$\Omega^{\bar m}$ and numerators
\[
 \mathcal N_j^m\Omega^{\bar m-m}\quad(0\le j\le2),
 \qquad
 \mathcal N_3^n\Omega^{\bar m-n}.
\]
All these numerators have degree at most $247\bar m$.
The original node coordinates, written with the same denominator,
have numerators of degree at most $244\bar m+1$.
It follows that multiplying a polynomial of degree at most $t$ in
the graph coordinates by $\Omega^{\bar mt}$ gives a polynomial in the
original nodes of degree at most $(247\bar m+1)t$.
This operation is injective on the corresponding spaces of functions,
because $\Omega$ is nonzero in $\mathbb Q(C)$.
Comparing their eventual affine Hilbert functions gives
\[
 \delta t+O(1)\le d(247\bar m+1)t+O(1),
\]
and hence the asserted upper bound.
In the unpowered case use $\Omega$ directly: the four new
numerators have degree at most $247$, and the original coordinates
have numerators of degree at most $245$.  The same comparison gives
$\delta\le247d$.
\end{proof}

Every good start whose free-node triple lies on $C$ gives an integer point on this graph.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:jointheights}, its coordinates have absolute values
at most
\[
 \max(3H,S^m,Y^n).
\]
The original node coordinates also show that distinct such node
triples remain distinct after adjoining the powers.

\subsection{The window bound and the final exponents}\label{sec:jointcount}

We use the coefficient-uniform affine-curve theorem of
Castryck--Cluckers--Dittmann--Nguyen~\cite[Theorem~3]{CCDN}, also stated as
\cite[Corollary~2.2]{CDHNV}: an integral affine curve $C$ over $\mathbb Q$ of degree $\delta$
in $\mathbb A^r$, with $r\ge2$ fixed, has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jointexternal}
 N_{\rm aff}(C,B)\ll_r \delta^3 B^{1/\delta}(\log B+\delta),\qquad B\ge1,
\end{equation}
integer points in the box $[-B,B]^r$, with an implied constant
independent of the coefficients of $C$. Its formal proof is discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:lean}.

\begin{proposition}[Window bound]\label{prop:jointwindow}
Fix positive integers $m,n$ and $\eta>0$, and put $\mathcal B=\max(H,S^m,Y^n)$.
For a window run in $[A,2A)$ with $T\ge36$ satisfying~\eqref{eq:jointscale},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jointcount}
 T\ll_{m,n,\eta}a^\eta\left(
 X^5+X^4H^{1/2}+X^5\mathcal B^{1/\delta_{\min}}\right).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
At least $T/2$ starts are good, and for each pattern the free-node triples of
its good starts are distinct (Lemma~\ref{lem:jointcuts}). By
Lemma~\ref{lem:jointcurve}, these triples lie on at most twenty-four integral
curves of degree at most twenty-four, and we bound the good starts on each
curve, allowing overlaps. All the heights $H$, $S$, $Y$ and $X$ are at most a fixed
power of $a$, so the logarithm in~\eqref{eq:jointexternal} is $O_\eta(a^\eta)$.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Exceptional patterns.} There are $O_\eta(a^\eta X^4)$ of them
(Lemma~\ref{lem:jointcomponents}). Under~\eqref{eq:jointscale} no line
contains a good start, so every relevant curve has degree $2\le\delta\le24$, and
\eqref{eq:jointexternal} in $\mathbb A^3$ with $B=3H$ gives $O_\eta(a^\eta H^{1/2})$
good starts per pattern. This gives the term $X^4H^{1/2}$.
\item \emph{Nonexceptional patterns: components that are not retained, and
conics.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:jointcuts} and Section~\ref{sec:jointconic},
these contribute $O_\eta(a^\eta)$ per pattern, hence $O_\eta(a^\eta X^5)$ in all.
\item \emph{Nonexceptional patterns: the remaining components.} Lines do not
occur, by Lemma~\ref{lem:jointcomponents}, and odd degrees do not occur, by
Corollary~\ref{cor:jointparity}, so these components have degree $d\ge4$.
Their good starts give distinct integer points of $\Gamma_{m,n}\subseteq\mathbb A^7$ in a box of
side $\max(3H,S^m,Y^n)\le3\mathcal B$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:jointpowereddegree}, the degree
$\delta$ of $\Gamma_{m,n}$ satisfies $\delta_{\min}\le\delta\le24(247(m+n)+1)$, so
\eqref{eq:jointexternal} gives $O_{m,n,\eta}(a^\eta\mathcal B^{1/\delta_{\min}})$
good starts per component. This gives the term $X^5\mathcal B^{1/\delta_{\min}}$.
\end{itemize}
The added coordinates $W_j$ and $J$ are functions of the free nodes on each
component. They therefore introduce no further freely counted coordinates, and
the pattern count is not enlarged.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:jointexponent}
For fixed positive integers $m,n$ and every $\varepsilon>0$,
\[
 P(a,b)\ll_{m,n,\varepsilon}a^{E(m,n)+\varepsilon},\qquad
 E(m,n)=\max\left(\frac{15\delta_{\min}+m}{51\delta_{\min}+3m},\
           \frac{15\delta_{\min}+6n}{51\delta_{\min}+20n}\right),
\]
where $\delta_{\min}=10n+7m$. In particular $E(1,1)=128/435$ and
$E(50,51)=2201/7480$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
We bound the number $T$ of terms in each dyadic window, eliminating the
window scale $A$. If $T<36$ there is nothing to prove. If $AT>28a$, then
Proposition~\ref{prop:six} gives $T^4\ll a$. Otherwise
\[
 HAT\le56a,\quad Xa^3\le3609600 A^4H^6,\quad T^2\le8\Delta A,
\]
the last by Proposition~\ref{prop:drop}. If~\eqref{eq:jointscale} fails, these give
$T^{17}\ll\Delta^3a^5$. If~\eqref{eq:jointscale} holds, we use
Proposition~\ref{prop:jointwindow}. The first two terms of~\eqref{eq:jointcount}
give $T^{51}\ll a^{15+O(\eta)}$ and $T^{85}\ll a^{25+O(\eta)}$, after absorbing
divisor factors. All these exponents equal $5/17$ up to the arbitrarily small loss.

Suppose the graph term dominates. Raising the bound to the power $\delta_{\min}$
gives $T^{\delta_{\min}}\le KX^{5\delta_{\min}}\mathcal B$,
where $K\ll_{m,n,\eta}a^{\eta\delta_{\min}}$ includes the counting loss. Suppose
also that $\mathcal B T^{e_T}\le C_1a^{e_a}$, where $C_1$ may include divisor
factors. Raising these inequalities to suitable powers and cancelling positive
powers of $A$ and $a$ gives
\[
 T^{51\delta_{\min}+e_T}\le
 K\,3609600^{5\delta_{\min}}56^{30\delta_{\min}}C_1(8\Delta)^{10\delta_{\min}}a^{15\delta_{\min}+e_a}.
\]
It remains to find $(e_T,e_a)$. The definitions of $S$ and $H$ with $HAT\le56a$
give $ST^3\ll a$, and $HT^3\le448\Delta a$ follows from the first and third
window inequalities. Since $H\ge1$ and $m\ge1$,
$\max(H,S^m)\le\max(H,S)^m$. So if $\mathcal B=\max(H,S^m)$ we may take
$(e_T,e_a)=(3m,m)$, up to divisor factors. The definition of $Y$ gives
$YT^{20}\ll\Delta^5a^6$, so if $\mathcal B=Y^n$ we may take
$(e_T,e_a)=(20n,6n)$.

Both fractions in $E(m,n)$ exceed $5/17$, and $T^4\ll a$ is stronger still.
After fixing $m,n$, all divisor and logarithmic losses are $O(a^\eta)$ for
every $\eta>0$. So every window has $T\ll a^{E(m,n)+\varepsilon/2}$, and summing
over the $O(\log a)$ windows proves the bound. For $m=n=1$ the two fractions
are $256/870=128/435$ and $261/887$, and the first is larger. For
$(m,n)=(50,51)$ the larger fraction is $2201/7480$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:joint}]
Set $r=n/m$. The two fractions in $E(m,n)$ become
\[
 E_S(r)=\frac{150r+106}{510r+360},\qquad
 E_Y(r)=\frac{156r+105}{530r+357}.
\]
The first decreases and the second increases on $r>0$. Their crossing satisfies
$30r^2-10r-21=0$, so $r_*=(5+\sqrt{655})/30$ and their common value is
$\alpha_*=(1310-\sqrt{655})/4365$.
Positive rational ratios approximate $r_*$; choose the fixed positive integers
$m,n$ to make $E(m,n)<\alpha_*+\varepsilon/2$, and apply
Corollary~\ref{cor:jointexponent} with loss $\varepsilon/2$.
The powers and all constants depend only on $\varepsilon$, not on $a,b$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
In terms of the scales, at the critical point $T\approx a^{\alpha_*}$ one has
$A\approx a^{2\alpha_*}$, $H\approx S\approx a^{1-3\alpha_*}\approx a^{0.117}$,
$X\approx a^{3-10\alpha_*}\approx a^{0.057}$ and
$Y\approx a^{6-20\alpha_*}\approx a^{0.115}$. The pattern count $X^5\approx a^{0.287}$
alone would give the exponent $5/17$. The graph term multiplies it by
$\mathcal B^{1/\delta_{\min}}$, and at $n/m\approx r_*$ the two box radii $S^m$ and $Y^n$
balance, at $\mathcal B\approx a^{0.117m}$. This gives the extra factor
$a^{0.117/(10r_*+7)}\approx a^{0.0068}$.
\end{remark}

\section{The Lean formalisation}\label{sec:lean}

The accompanying Lean~4 project uses toolchain \lean{v4.35.0} and Mathlib
\lean{v4.35.0}. It proves the main results of this paper, including the
curve-counting input to Theorem~\ref{thm:joint}. The logarithmic refinement
is the one exception: its Lean proof takes the two published analytic
estimates \eqref{eq:NT} and~\eqref{eq:BT} as explicit hypotheses.

The strongest bound has the following statement:
\begin{quote}\ttfamily\small
theorem P\_le\_joint\_optimal :\\
\hspace*{2em}$\forall$ $\varepsilon$ > (0 : $\mathbb R$), $\exists$ C > 0, $\forall$ a b : $\mathbb N$,\\
\hspace*{2em}0 < b $\to$ b < a $\to$ (P a b : $\mathbb R$) $\le$\\
\hspace*{2em}C * (a : $\mathbb R$) \^{} (((1310 : $\mathbb R$) - Real.sqrt 655) / 4365 + $\varepsilon$)
\end{quote}
The order of the quantifiers makes the uniformity explicit: $C$ may depend
on $\varepsilon$, but not on $a$ or $b$. Here \lean{P a b} is defined by
well-founded recursion,
\[
 P(a,0)=0,\qquad P(a,b)=1+P(a,a\bmod b)\quad(b>0),
\]
so it counts exactly the positive terms of the orbit. The formal orbit is
indexed from zero, as in this paper.

\section{Limits of the method}\label{sec:limits}

\emph{The threshold $5/17$.} In the proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:jointexponent}, three
contributions give exactly the exponent $5/17$, up to the arbitrarily small loss: the
pattern count $X^5$ (from components that are not retained and from conics), the
exceptional patterns, and windows in which the line-exclusion
condition~\eqref{eq:jointscale} fails. None of them involves the added coordinates $W$
and $J$. So improving
the coordinate embedding alone cannot go below $5/17=0.2941176\ldots$, which $\alpha_*$
exceeds by about $1.3\cdot10^{-4}$. A stronger degree bound for the powered graphs may
bring the exponent closer to $5/17$; going below it requires a sharper count of the
patterns that actually occur along an orbit. No claim of optimality for the Pierce
iteration is made.

\emph{The earlier arguments.} Proposition~\ref{prop:six} removes the divisor loss from the local bound, but on its own the
exponent is still $3/10$. The local bound $T\ll a^{1/2}A^{-1/3}$ and the drop bound $T\ll A^{1/2}$ (up to
the divisor factor) both allow $T\asymp a^{3/10}$ at $A\asymp a^{3/5}$. Even an ideal
short-interval divisor estimate with $G$ constant leaves this balance unchanged. So sharpening
constants or improving the divisor counts in that argument cannot give a logarithmic bound.
To improve that argument, one needs to restrict \emph{which} configurations of the parameters $(u,v,w,z,D,E,F)$ can occur
together along a single orbit. Theorem~\ref{thm:rises} is one restriction of this kind: a long
orbit must have many rises in its quotient increments. Theorem~\ref{thm:hankel} supplies the
natural candidates for further ones, the higher invariants $I_r$. Section~\ref{sec:2377} uses
$I_4$ in exactly this way.

The count of Section~\ref{sec:1137} loses through the number of patterns, $X^7$, reduced to
$X^5$ in Section~\ref{sec:827} and weighted by the lattices $\Lambda_p$ in
Section~\ref{sec:45152}. With patterns at four consecutive states one has thirteen integers and
as many equations as unknown increments, so one might hope for $O(1)$ configurations per
pattern. But the four-state equations have
positive-dimensional families of solutions. For instance, the Newton coefficients
$(\alpha,-3\alpha-1,3\alpha+3,-\alpha-3,1)$, with coefficient polynomial $(z-1)^3(z-\alpha)$, at
every state, together with constant increments $k_j=t$, satisfy all the linear and quadratic
relations for every $t$. Such families are excluded only by the remainder equations, which those earlier
counts discard. The fixed-dividend curve equations of Section~\ref{sec:joint}
retain this information. Section~\ref{sec:45152} uses only the first minimum of $\Lambda_p$, and slices in a
single direction; the other two minima and the shape of the quadric in the lattice are not used.

There is no obvious monotonicity to exploit. The curvatures $D_i$ of successive blocks need
not decrease. For $a=13242$ the orbit of $255$ begins
\[
255,\ 237,\ 207,\ 201,\ 177,\ 144,\ 138,\ 132,\ \dots
\]
with quotients $51,55,63,65,74,91,95,100,\dots$. The curvatures of the first five blocks are
$24,12,6,111,30$, all for blocks that lie within a factor of two.
