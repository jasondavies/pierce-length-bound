\title[A discrete iteration related to Pierce expansions]{The iteration $x\mapsto a \bmod x$ has length $O_\varepsilon(a^{3/10+\varepsilon})$}

\author{Jason Davies}
\email{jason@jasondavies.com}

\date{October 2026}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
For integers $a>b>0$ let $b_1=b$, $b_{i+1}=a\bmod b_i$, and let $P(a,b)$ be the number of
positive terms of this sequence. Shallit asked whether $P(a,b)=O((\log a)^2)$. The bound
$P(a,b)\ll_\varepsilon a^{1/3+\varepsilon}$ of Erd\H{o}s and Shallit was improved by Chase and
Pandey to $a^{1/3-2/177+\varepsilon}$. We prove $P(a,b)\ll_\varepsilon a^{3/10+\varepsilon}$
by an elementary argument. The new ingredient is an exact identity for three consecutive steps
of the iteration. It shows that a certain integer combination $D=dv-eu$ of the drops and
quotient increments is always positive. It is also small whenever the three drops are small,
and $D$ together with the two increments confines the block to a curve with few integer points.
A second identity, for six consecutive steps, produces a further positive integer $L$ built from
three overlapping curvatures. It gives the local bound $T\ll a^{1/2}A^{-1/3}$ for the number $T$
of remainders in $[A,2A)$, with no divisor-function loss. Combined with results of Nair--Tenenbaum
and de la Bret\`eche--Tenenbaum on the Erd\H{o}s--Hooley $\Delta$-function, it gives
$P(a,b)\ll a^{3/10}\sqrt{\log\log 3a}$. We also show that $P(a,b)\ll a^{1/4}\sqrt R$, where
$R-1$ is the number of rises in the sequence of quotient increments. Finally, $D$ and $L$ are the
third and fourth members of an infinite hierarchy of positive integers: Hankel determinants of
an exact positive moment model of the orbit. Everything is formalised in Lean~4 with Mathlib,
without \lean{sorry}. The $\sqrt{\log\log 3a}$ bound is formalised conditionally on the two
cited theorems, which are stated in Lean as explicit hypotheses.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Let $a>b>0$ be integers. Put $b_1=b$ and $b_{i+1}=a \bmod b_i$ while $b_i>0$. The sequence is
strictly decreasing until it reaches $0$; if $b_{n+1}=0$ we write $P(a,b)=n$. For example
$P(35,22)=7$, from $22,13,9,8,3,2,1,0$. The number $P(a,b)$ is the length of the Pierce
expansion of $b/a$~\cite{Pierce,Shallit86}, since
\[
\frac ba=\frac1{q_1}-\frac1{q_1q_2}+\frac1{q_1q_2q_3}-\cdots,\qquad q_i=\lfloor a/b_i\rfloor .
\]
Shallit~\cite{OPG} asked whether
\begin{equation}\label{eq:conj}
P(a,b)=O\bigl((\log a)^2\bigr).
\end{equation}

Shallit~\cite{Shallit86} proved $P(a,b)\ll a^{1/2}$ by ``Archimedean'' arguments.
Erd\H{o}s and Shallit~\cite{ES} combined these with a divisor argument to get
$P(a,b)\ll_\varepsilon a^{1/3+\varepsilon}$, and showed that $\max_b P(a,b)/\log a$ does not
tend to $0$. Chase and Pandey~\cite{CP} went past the exponent $1/3$, proving
$P(a,b)\ll_\varepsilon a^{1/3-2/177+\varepsilon}$ with exponential sum estimates
(Bourgain's exponent pair), and showed that $\max_b P(a,b)\gg \log a/\log\log a$ for all large
$a$. Shallit's talk pages~\cite{ShallitTalks} report a bound $n^{3/10}$, described as
``LLM, unpublished so far''. We have not seen that argument. The result below has the same
exponent up to $\varepsilon$, and we make no claim about how the two arguments are related.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
For every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a constant $C_\varepsilon$ such that
\[
P(a,b)\le C_\varepsilon\, a^{3/10+\varepsilon}\qquad\text{for all integers }0<b<a .
\]
\end{theorem}

The proof is elementary. It uses only the division equations
$a=q_ib_i+b_{i+1}$ and the divisor bound $\tau(n)\ll_\delta n^\delta$. As usual, the remainders
are split into dyadic windows $[A,2A)$, and we bound the number $T$ of remainders in one
window. There are two bounds:
\begin{align}
T(T-1)&\le 4A\,\Delta, \label{eq:intro1}\\
T^6A^2&\le 995328\, a^3\Delta \qquad(T\ge 12), \label{eq:intro2}
\end{align}
where $\Delta$ is the largest value of the divisor function on $[1,32a^7]$.
Bound~\eqref{eq:intro1} is the Erd\H{o}s--Shallit argument. Bound~\eqref{eq:intro2} is new.
Multiplying~\eqref{eq:intro2} by the square of~\eqref{eq:intro1} eliminates $A$ and gives
$T^{10}\ll a^3\Delta^3$, which is the exponent $3/10$.

The new bound comes from three consecutive steps $x=b_i$, $y=b_{i+1}$, $z=b_{i+2}$,
$w=b_{i+3}$. Let $d,e,f$ be the drops, let $u,v$ be the increments of the quotients, and put
$D=dv-eu$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intro-curv}
D\,z=df+ude+(u-1)e^2 .
\end{equation}
So $D$ is a \emph{positive} integer, even though $dv$ and $eu$ largely cancel, and it is small
when the drops are small. Also $(q,x)=(\lfloor a/b_i\rfloor,b_i)$ lies on the curve
$xM(q)=aN(q)+D$, where $M$ and $N$ are explicit polynomials depending on $(u,v)$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:fiber-eq}). A resultant-type identity shows that each such curve has
$a^{o(1)}$ admissible points (Lemma~\ref{lem:fiber}). The number of triples $(u,v,D)$ is
$\ll (B/T)^2\cdot a/T^3$, where $B=a/A$, which gives~\eqref{eq:intro2}.

\subsection*{A second positive integer}
Six consecutive terms carry three overlapping curvatures $D,E,F$ and four quotient increments
$u,v,w,z$. In Section~\ref{sec:six} we show that the integer
\[
L=\frac{S(v,w,z)\,D-S(u,v,w)\,E}{v},\qquad S(u,v,w)=u^2v+(u-1)v^2+uw,
\]
satisfies the exact identity $qwL=J(u,v,w,z)\,E+S(u,v,w)\,F$, where $J$ is a polynomial with
positive values (Lemma~\ref{lem:six}). Hence $L\ge1$, and $q\asymp a/A$ is bounded by a
polynomial in the small parameters $u,v,w,z,E,F$. A single window of six terms with small total
drop then gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intro3}
T^6A^2\le 96\cdot10^6\,a^3\qquad(T\ge10),
\end{equation}
with no divisor function at all (Proposition~\ref{prop:six}).

With~\eqref{eq:intro3} in hand, the only remaining loss is in the drop bound~\eqref{eq:intro1}. That
loss comes from the number of divisors of $a+h$ in $[A,2A)$ as $h$ ranges over a short interval.
Section~\ref{sec:short} shows that any bound $\ll y\,G(x)$ for such counts over intervals
$(x,x+y]$ with $y\ge x^{1/4}$ gives $P(a,b)\ll a^{3/10}G(a)^{1/5}$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:generic}).
The counts are majorised by the Erd\H{o}s--Hooley function. Combining the short-sum theorem of
Nair and Tenenbaum~\cite{NT} with the recent mean-value bound of de la Bret\`eche and
Tenenbaum~\cite{BT} gives $G(x)=(\log\log x)^{5/2}$, and hence the following.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:loglog}
Assume the theorem of Nair--Tenenbaum stated as~\eqref{eq:NT} and the theorem of de la
Bret\`eche--Tenenbaum stated as~\eqref{eq:BT} below. Then
\[
P(a,b)\ll a^{3/10}\sqrt{\log\log 3a}\qquad\text{uniformly for integers }0<b<a .
\]
\end{theorem}

Both hypotheses are published theorems. They are hypotheses only in the sense of the
formalisation: we have not formalised their proofs. Any other short-interval divisor bound
can be used in their place. For example, Shiu's theorem~\cite{Shiu} gives
$\sum_{x<n\le x+y}\tau(n)\ll y\log x$ for $y\ge x^{1/4}$, and hence
$P(a,b)\ll a^{3/10}(\log a)^{1/5}$; we have not formalised that either.

\subsection*{Structured trajectories and the Hankel hierarchy}
Write $k_i=q_{i+1}-q_i$ for the quotient increments, and let $R-1$ be the number of indices with
$k_i<k_{i+1}$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:rises}
$P(a,b)\ll a^{1/4}\sqrt R$ uniformly for $0<b<a$. In particular, if the quotient increments
never rise, then $P(a,b)\ll a^{1/4}$.
\end{theorem}

The proof (Section~\ref{sec:rises}) combines three facts. First, $D\ge1$ says that $d_i/k_i$ is
strictly decreasing, so the drops strictly decrease along every stretch without a rise. Second,
the six-step integer $L$ gives an exact identity for six quotients in arithmetic progression,
which forces their common difference to be large (Lemma~\ref{lem:ap}). Third, a counting lemma
for sequences in which no value occurs too often. So an orbit of length about $a^{3/10}$ must
contain $\gg a^{1/10}$ rises.

Finally, both $D$ and $L$ fit into a single structure (Section~\ref{sec:hankel}). The orbit
carries an exact finite model by positive measures $\nu_i$ with $\int d\nu_i=b_i/a$. The Hankel
determinants $I_r(i)=a\det\bigl(\int x^{j+l}\,d\nu_i\bigr)_{j,l<r}$ are positive integers, and
$I_1,I_2,I_3,I_4$ are $b_i$, $b_i-b_{i+1}$, $D$ and $L$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:hankel}).

\subsection*{Formal verification}
Every step of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} has been formalised in Lean~4~\cite{Lean}
using Mathlib~\cite{Mathlib}. So have Proposition~\ref{prop:six}, Theorem~\ref{thm:generic},
the deduction of Theorem~\ref{thm:loglog} from~\eqref{eq:NT} and~\eqref{eq:BT},
Theorem~\ref{thm:rises} and Theorem~\ref{thm:hankel}.
Section~\ref{sec:lean} describes the formalisation. The constants in this paper are the
constants in the Lean proof.

\subsection*{What is not proved}
These results are far from~\eqref{eq:conj}, and the method cannot reach it; see
Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\section{Preliminaries}

\subsection{Notation}
Throughout, $a>b>0$ are integers, $b_1=b$, $b_{i+1}=a\bmod b_i$, and $P=P(a,b)$, so that
$a>b_1>b_2>\dots>b_P>0=b_{P+1}$. Write $q_i=\lfloor a/b_i\rfloor$, so
\[
a=q_ib_i+b_{i+1},\qquad 0\le b_{i+1}<b_i\qquad(1\le i\le P).
\]
Let $\tau(n)$ be the number of positive divisors of $n$, and let
\[
\Delta=\Delta(a)=\max_{1\le n\le 32a^7}\tau(n).
\]

\begin{lemma}[Quotients increase]\label{lem:quot}
If $b_{i+1}>0$ then $q_{i+1}\ge q_i+1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $b_{i+1}<b_i$ we have $(q_i+1)b_{i+1}\le q_ib_i+b_{i+1}=a$, so
$q_{i+1}=\lfloor a/b_{i+1}\rfloor\ge q_i+1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Divisor bound]\label{lem:div}
For every integer $k\ge1$ there is $C_k$ with $\tau(n)^k\le C_k\,n$ for all $n\ge1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
This is standard: $\tau(n)^k/n=\prod_{p^\alpha\| n}(\alpha+1)^k/p^\alpha$. Each factor with
$p\ge 2^k$ is at most $(\alpha+1)^k/2^{k\alpha}\le1$. Each of the fewer than $2^k$ factors with
$p<2^k$ is at most $\sup_\alpha(\alpha+1)^k/2^\alpha<\infty$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Window runs}

\begin{definition}
Let $A\ge1$. A \emph{window run} of length $T$ is a sequence $x_0,x_1,\dots,x_{T-1}$ of
consecutive terms of the sequence $(b_i)$, so $x_{j+1}=a\bmod x_j$, all lying in $[A,2A)$.
\end{definition}

In a window run, $x_0>x_1>\dots>x_{T-1}$ and $x_j\le a$.

\section{The curvature of three consecutive steps}

Fix four consecutive terms
\[
x=b_i,\quad y=b_{i+1}=a\bmod x,\quad z=b_{i+2}=a\bmod y,\quad w=b_{i+3}=a\bmod z,
\]
with $x,y,z>0$ (and $w\ge0$). Write
\[
q=\left\lfloor \frac ax\right\rfloor,\quad
u=\left\lfloor \frac ay\right\rfloor-q,\quad
v=\left\lfloor \frac az\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor \frac ay\right\rfloor,\qquad
d=x-y,\quad e=y-z,\quad f=z-w .
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:quot}, $u,v\ge1$, and clearly $d,e,f\ge1$. The division equations read
\begin{equation}\label{eq:div}
xq+y=a,\qquad y(q+u)+z=a,\qquad z(q+u+v)+w=a .
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}
The \emph{curvature} of the block is $D=dv-eu$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Curvature identity]\label{lem:curv}
\[
D\,z=df+ude+(u-1)e^2 .
\]
In particular $D\ge1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Subtracting the first two equations of~\eqref{eq:div} gives $uy=qd+e$, hence
$uz=uy-ue=qd+(1-u)e$. Subtracting the last two gives $vz=(q+u)e+f$. Therefore
\[
(dv-eu)z=d\bigl((q+u)e+f\bigr)-e\bigl(qd+(1-u)e\bigr)=df+ude+(u-1)e^2 .
\]
The right-hand side is at least $df\ge1$, and $z>0$, so $D>0$.
\end{proof}

Equivalently, the identity is the linear combination
\[
Dz-\bigl(df+ude+(u-1)e^2\bigr)=d\,E_3+(a-qx-x-y+z)\,E_2+(-a+(q+u)y+y)\,E_1
\]
of the three division equations $E_1,E_2,E_3$ of~\eqref{eq:div} (each written as
$\text{left}-\text{right}=0$). This is how it is checked in Lean.

\begin{lemma}[The fiber equation]\label{lem:fiber-eq}
Put $M(q)=uq^2+(u^2+u+v)q+v$ and $N(q)=uq+u^2+v$. Then
\[
x\,M(q)=a\,N(q)+D .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
From~\eqref{eq:div}, $d=(q+1)x-a$ and $e=y-z=(q+u+1)y-a=a(q+u)-q(q+u+1)x$. Substituting
these into $D=dv-eu$ gives
$D=x\bigl(uq(q+u+1)+v(q+1)\bigr)-a\bigl(u(q+u)+v\bigr)$, which is the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Size of the parameters]\label{lem:size}
If $x\le a$ then
\[
u\,yx\le a(d+e),\qquad v\,zy\le a(e+f),\qquad D\,z\le (u+1)(d+e+f)^2 .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
From $uy=qd+e$ we get $uyx=(qx)d+ex\le ad+ae$, since $qx\le a$ and $x\le a$. Similarly
$vz=(q+u)e+f$ with $(q+u)y\le a$ and $y\le a$ gives the second bound. For the third, by
Lemma~\ref{lem:curv},
$Dz=df+ude+(u-1)e^2\le df+u\,e(d+e)\le (1+u)(d+e+f)^2$.
\end{proof}

\section{Few blocks share a triple $(u,v,D)$}

\begin{lemma}[Fiber count]\label{lem:fiber}
Let $a\ge1$, $1\le u,v\le a$ and $1\le D\le 2a$. Let $M(q)=uq^2+pq+v$ with $p=u^2+u+v$, and
let $L(q)=cq+\ell$ with $c=au$ and $\ell=a(u^2+v)+D$. Then the set
\[
Q=\{q\ge 0:\ xM(q)=L(q)\text{ for some integer }x\ge1\}
\]
has at most $\Delta=\max_{n\le 32a^7}\tau(n)$ elements.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $U(q)=ucq+cp-u\ell$ and $R=u\ell^2-cp\ell+c^2v$. Expanding shows
\begin{equation}\label{eq:resultant}
c^2M(q)-U(q)L(q)=R
\end{equation}
identically in $q$. If $q\in Q$ with witness $x$, substituting $L(q)=xM(q)$
into~\eqref{eq:resultant} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:R}
R=M(q)\bigl(c^2-U(q)x\bigr).
\end{equation}
We bound the sizes first. From $u,v\le a$ and $D\le 2a$ we get $\ell\le 4a^3$, $p\le 3a^2$
and $c\le a^2$, so
\[
|R|\le u\ell^2+cp\ell+c^2v\le 16a^7+12a^7+a^5\le 32a^7,\qquad c^2\le a^4\le 32a^7 .
\]

\emph{Case $R\ne0$.} By~\eqref{eq:R}, $M(q)$ is a positive divisor of $|R|$. Since $u,p\ge1$,
the map $q\mapsto M(q)$ is strictly increasing on $q\ge0$, hence injective. Therefore
$|Q|\le\tau(|R|)\le\Delta$.

\emph{Case $R=0$.} Since $M(q)>0$, \eqref{eq:R} gives $U(q)x=c^2$. Here $c^2>0$ and $x\ge1$,
so $U(q)$ is a positive divisor of $c^2$. Since $U$ is linear with positive leading
coefficient $uc$, it is injective. Therefore $|Q|\le\tau(c^2)\le\Delta$.
\end{proof}

The case $R=0$ cannot be dropped: it is the case in which $M$ and $L$ might share a factor.

\section{Counting visits to a window}

In this section $x_0,\dots,x_{T-1}$ is a window run in $[A,2A)$, and
$\Delta=\max_{n\le 32a^7}\tau(n)$.

\begin{proposition}[Drop bound; after Erd\H{o}s--Shallit]\label{prop:drop}
If $T\ge2$ then $T(T-1)\le 4A\Delta$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $h_j=x_j-x_{j+1}\ge1$ for $0\le j\le T-2$. By telescoping,
$\sum_j h_j=x_0-x_{T-1}\le (2A-1)-A=A-1$. Call $j$ \emph{good} if $h_j(T-1)\le 2A$ and
\emph{bad} otherwise. Each bad $j$ has $h_j(T-1)\ge 2A+1$, so
\[
|\Bad|\,(2A+1)\le (T-1)\textstyle\sum_j h_j\le (T-1)(A-1).
\]
If $2|\Bad|\ge T-1$ this would give $(T-1)(2A+1)\le 2(T-1)(A-1)$, which is false. Hence
$2|\Bad|<T-1$, and since $|\Good|+|\Bad|=T-1$ we get $2|\Good|\ge T$.

For good $j$ we have $1\le h_j\le H:=\lfloor 2A/(T-1)\rfloor$. Also
$a+h_j=q_jx_j+x_{j+1}+x_j-x_{j+1}=(q_j+1)x_j$, so $x_j\mid a+h_j$. Moreover
$a+h_j\le 2a\le 32a^7$. Since $j\mapsto x_j$ is injective, at most $\tau(a+h)\le\Delta$ good
indices have $h_j=h$. Hence $|\Good|\le H\Delta$, and
\[
T(T-1)\le 2|\Good|(T-1)\le 2\Delta\,H(T-1)\le 4A\Delta. \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Curvature bound]\label{prop:curv}
If $T\ge12$ then $T^6A^2\le 995328\,a^3\Delta$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $0\le j\le T-4$ let $s_j=x_j-x_{j+3}$ be the total drop of the block
$x_j,x_{j+1},x_{j+2},x_{j+3}$. By telescoping,
\[
\sum_{j=0}^{T-4}s_j=(x_0+x_1+x_2)-(x_{T-3}+x_{T-2}+x_{T-1})\le 3(2A-1)-3A=3A-3 .
\]
Call $j$ \emph{good} if $s_jT\le 12A$ and \emph{bad} otherwise. Then
$|\Bad|(12A+1)\le T(3A-3)$. If $4|\Bad|\ge T$ this would give $T(12A+1)\le 4T(3A-3)$, which is
false. So $4|\Bad|\le T-1$. Since $|\Good|=T-3-|\Bad|$ and $T\ge12$,
\[
2|\Good|\ge 2T-6-\tfrac{T-1}2\ge T .
\]

Fix a good $j$. Apply Section~3 to $x=x_j$, so that $y,z,w=x_{j+1},x_{j+2},x_{j+3}$, all
in $[A,2A)$. Write $s=s_j=d+e+f$. Lemma~\ref{lem:size} gives $uA^2\le uyx\le a(d+e)\le as$.
Multiplying by $T$ and using $sT\le 12A$ gives $uA^2T\le 12aA$, that is,
\[
u\,AT\le 12a,\qquad\text{and likewise}\qquad v\,AT\le 12a .
\]
Also $DA\le Dz\le(u+1)s^2\le 2us^2$. Multiplying by $T^2$ gives
$DAT^2\le 2u(sT)^2\le 288uA^2$, so $DT^2\le 288uA$, and then
\[
D\,T^3\le 288\,u\,AT\le 3456\,a .
\]
Thus the triple $(u,v,D)$ of a good block lies in $[1,U]^2\times[1,D^*]$, where
\[
U=\lfloor 12a/(AT)\rfloor\le a,\qquad D^*=\lfloor 3456a/T^3\rfloor\le 2a .
\]
The inequalities $U\le a$ and $D^*\le 2a$ hold because $AT\ge12$ and $T^3\ge1728$.

Now fix such a triple and consider the good blocks that have it. By Lemma~\ref{lem:fiber-eq},
each one gives a point $q=q_j=\lfloor a/x_j\rfloor$ of the set $Q$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:fiber}.
The map $j\mapsto q_j$ is injective on these blocks: $q_j$ determines $x_j=L(q_j)/M(q_j)$, and
$x_j$ determines $j$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:fiber}, at most $\Delta$ good blocks share a triple.
So $|\Good|\le U^2D^*\Delta$, and
\[
T^6A^2=T\,(AT)^2\,T^3\le 2\Delta\,(U\,AT)^2\,(D^*T^3)\le 2\Delta(12a)^2(3456a)=995328\,a^3\Delta.
\qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Window bound]\label{cor:window}
If $T\ge12$ then $T^{10}\le 63700992\,a^3\Delta^3$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Using $T\le 2(T-1)$ and the two propositions,
\[
T^{10}A^2\le 4\,(T^6A^2)\,\bigl(T(T-1)\bigr)^2\le 4\cdot 995328\,a^3\Delta\cdot(4A\Delta)^2
=63700992\,a^3\Delta^3A^2 .
\]
Divide by $A^2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
In terms of $B=a/A$, Propositions~\ref{prop:drop} and~\ref{prop:curv} say
$T\ll a^{1/2+o(1)}B^{-1/2}$ and $T\ll a^{1/6+o(1)}B^{1/3}$. The first is better when
$B>a^{2/5}$, and both equal $a^{3/10+o(1)}$ at $B=a^{2/5}$. Corollary~\ref{cor:window} takes
the product of the two bounds, so no case split is needed.
\end{remark}

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}}

Let $0<b<a$. For $0\le k\le\log_2 a$ let $T_k$ be the number of indices $i\le P$ with
$2^k\le b_i<2^{k+1}$. Every $b_i$ with $i\le P$ lies in $[1,a)$, so
$P=\sum_{0\le k\le \log_2 a}T_k$. Since $(b_i)$ is decreasing, the indices counted by $T_k$
are consecutive. So when $T_k>0$ they form a window run in $[A,2A)$ with $A=2^k$.

Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose an integer $m>7/(5\varepsilon)$, and let $C_{3m}$ be the constant of
Lemma~\ref{lem:div} with $k=3m$. The maximum $\Delta$ is attained at some $n_0\le 32a^7$, so
$\Delta^{3m}\le C_{3m}\cdot 32a^7$. If $T_k\ge12$, Corollary~\ref{cor:window} gives
\[
T_k^{10m}\le 63700992^m\,a^{3m}\,\Delta^{3m}\le K\,a^{3m+7},\qquad K=63700992^m\cdot C_{3m}\cdot 32,
\]
and hence
\[
T_k\le K^{1/(10m)}\,a^{3/10+7/(10m)}\le K^{1/(10m)}\,a^{3/10+\varepsilon/2}.
\]
If $T_k\le11$ this bound holds with $11$ in place of $K^{1/(10m)}$. Finally
$\lfloor\log_2a\rfloor+1\le C'_\varepsilon a^{\varepsilon/2}$. Summing over $k$,
\[
P(a,b)\le (\lfloor\log_2 a\rfloor+1)\bigl(11+K^{1/(10m)}\bigr)a^{3/10+\varepsilon/2}
\le C_\varepsilon\, a^{3/10+\varepsilon}. \qquad\qed
\]

\section{Six consecutive steps}\label{sec:six}

Fix six consecutive terms $x_0>x_1>\dots>x_5\ge0$ of the sequence $(b_i)$ with $x_4>0$, and
write their quotients as
\begin{gather*}
\lfloor a/x_0\rfloor=q,\quad \lfloor a/x_1\rfloor=q+u,\quad \lfloor a/x_2\rfloor=q+u+v,\\
\lfloor a/x_3\rfloor=q+u+v+w,\quad \lfloor a/x_4\rfloor=q+u+v+w+z .
\end{gather*}
(In this section $w$ is a quotient increment, not a term of the sequence.) By
Lemma~\ref{lem:quot}, $u,v,w,z\ge1$. Let $f_k=x_k-x_{k+1}\ge1$ be the drops, and let
\[
D=f_0v-f_1u,\qquad E=f_1w-f_2v,\qquad F=f_2z-f_3w
\]
be the curvatures of the blocks starting at $x_0$, $x_1$ and $x_2$. Write $E_k$ for the division
equation $x_kq_k+x_{k+1}-a=0$, where $q_k=\lfloor a/x_k\rfloor$. Put
\begin{align*}
S(u,v,w)&=u^2v+(u-1)v^2+uw,\\
J(u,v,w,z)&=\bigl(u^2+(u-1)v\bigr)z+w\bigl(u^3+uv(2u-1)+(u-1)v^2\bigr)+w^2\bigl((u-1)(u+v)+1\bigr),\\
L&=(vw+w^2+z)\,D-\bigl(u^2+(u-1)v\bigr)E-w\,(wf_0-uf_2).
\end{align*}

\begin{lemma}[The six-step identity]\label{lem:six}
\begin{enumerate}
\item $S(v,w,z)\,D-S(u,v,w)\,E=v\,L$.
\item $q\,w\,L=J(u,v,w,z)\,E+S(u,v,w)\,F$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Part~(1) is a polynomial identity: since $S(v,w,z)\equiv-w^2$ and $S(u,v,w)\equiv uw\pmod v$,
and $wD+uE=v(wf_0-uf_2)$, the difference is divisible by $v$, and expanding gives $L$. Part~(2)
holds modulo the division equations. Explicitly, $qwL-JE-S(u,v,w)F$ equals
\begin{align*}
&w(v^2w+vw^2+vz-w^2)\,E_0-w(u^2w+2uvw+uw^2+uz+v^2w+vw^2-vw+vz-w^2)\,E_1\\
&+w(u^2v+u^2w+uv^2+2uvw+uw^2+uw+uz-v^2-vw)\,E_2-w(u^2v+uv^2+uw-v^2)\,E_3 .
\end{align*}
This is an identity of polynomials in $a$, $q$, the $x_k$ and the increments, checked by expansion.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:six}
Suppose $q\ge1$ and $u,v,w,z\le m$. Then $E,F\ge1$, $L\ge1$, and
\[
q\le 9m^3E+3m^3F .
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Lemma~\ref{lem:curv}, applied to the blocks starting at $x_1$ and $x_2$, gives $E,F\ge1$.
Clearly $J>0$ and $S\ge0$, so by Lemma~\ref{lem:six}(2) $qwL>0$. Since $L$ is an integer,
$L\ge1$ and $qw\le JE+SF$. Bounding each monomial gives $J\le 9m^3w$ and $S(u,v,w)\le 3m^3w$.
Dividing by $w$ gives the claim.
\end{proof}

So the quotient $q$, which is about $a/A$, is controlled by the small parameters of a single block
of six terms. No counting over many blocks is needed.

\begin{proposition}[Six-step window bound]\label{prop:six}
For a window run of length $T\ge10$,
\[
T^6A^2\le 96\cdot10^6\,a^3 .
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $0\le j\le T-6$ let $s_j=x_j-x_{j+5}$. By telescoping,
$\sum_{j\le T-6}s_j\le 5(2A-1)-5A=5A-5$. So some $j$ has $s_j(T-5)\le 5A-5$, and since
$T\le 2(T-5)$, we get $s:=s_j$ with $sT\le 10A$. Apply Corollary~\ref{cor:six} to
$x_j,\dots,x_{j+5}$, all of which lie in $[A,2A)$, with $m=\max(u,v,w,z)$.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:size} at $x_j,\dots,x_{j+3}$, each increment satisfies
$(\text{increment})\cdot A^2\le a s$. Multiplying by $T$ gives $mAT\le 10a$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:size} again, $EA\le Ex_{j+3}\le(v+1)s^2\le 2ms^2$, and likewise $FA\le 2ms^2$.
Hence $qA\le 24m^4s^2$. Also $a=qx_j+x_{j+1}<(q+1)x_j<2A(q+1)\le 4Aq$. Therefore
\[
qA\cdot A^4T^6\le 24\,(mAT)^4(sT)^2\le 24\cdot10^6\,a^4A^2,
\]
so $qA^3T^6\le 24\cdot 10^6a^4$, and $aA^2T^6\le 4qA^3T^6\le 96\cdot10^6\,a^4$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
In terms of $B=a/A$, Proposition~\ref{prop:six} reads $T\ll a^{1/6}B^{1/3}$. This is
Proposition~\ref{prop:curv} without the factor $\Delta^{1/6}$. Combined with
Proposition~\ref{prop:drop} as in Corollary~\ref{cor:window}, it gives $T^{10}\ll a^3\Delta^2$
and another proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. The Lean proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} uses
Corollary~\ref{cor:window}.
\end{remark}

\section{Divisors over short intervals}\label{sec:short}

For $A\ge1$ let $\tau_A(n)=\#\{d\mid n:\ A\le d<2A\}$.

\begin{proposition}[Drop count]\label{prop:dropcount}
For a window run of length $T\ge2$ and any $Y\ge\lfloor 2A/(T-1)\rfloor$,
\[
T\le 2\sum_{a<n\le a+Y}\tau_A(n).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
This is the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:drop}, stopped before the divisor bound. The good
indices $j$ have $1\le h_j\le\lfloor 2A/(T-1)\rfloor$, and $j\mapsto x_j$ maps those with $h_j=h$
injectively into the divisors of $a+h$ in $[A,2A)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
Let $G\colon\mathbb N\to\mathbb R$. We say that $\mathrm{SD}(G)$ holds if there are $C,x_0$ such
that for all integers $x\ge x_0$, $x^{1/4}\le y\le x$ and $A\ge1$,
\[
\sum_{x<n\le x+y}\tau_A(n)\le C\,y\,G(x).
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:generic}
Suppose $\mathrm{SD}(G)$ holds, $G\ge1$, and $G(x)\ll x^{1/40}$. Then
$P(a,b)\ll a^{3/10}\,G(a)^{1/5}$ uniformly for $0<b<a$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Small $a$ are handled by $P(a,b)\le b<a$. Fix a dyadic window $[A,2A)$, $A=2^k$, with
$T\ge20$ visits. Proposition~\ref{prop:six} gives $T\le\alpha A^{-1/3}$, where
$\alpha=(96\cdot10^6)^{1/6}a^{1/2}$. Take $Y=\max(\lfloor 2A/(T-1)\rfloor,\lceil a^{1/4}\rceil)\le a$
in Proposition~\ref{prop:dropcount}. With $H=\lfloor 2A/(T-1)\rfloor$, $\mathrm{SD}(G)$ gives
$T\le 2CG(a)(H+a^{1/4}+1)$. Multiplying by $T-1$, using $H(T-1)\le2A$, and solving the quadratic
gives $T\le\gamma+\beta A^{1/2}$, where $\beta=4\sqrt{CG(a)}$ and $\gamma=4CG(a)(a^{1/4}+1)$. So
every window satisfies
\[
T_k\le 19+\gamma+\min\bigl(\alpha\,2^{-k/3},\ \beta\,2^{k/2}\bigr).
\]
Let $M=\alpha^{3/5}\beta^{2/5}$. Since $X^3Y^2=M^5$ for $X=\alpha2^{-k/3}$ and $Y=\beta2^{k/2}$,
for each $k$ either $Y\le M$ or $X<M$. So the minimum is at most the sum of a geometric series
with ratio $\sqrt2$ capped at $M$, plus one with ratio $2^{-1/3}$ capped at $M$. This gives
\[
\sum_{k}\min\bigl(\alpha\,2^{-k/3},\ \beta\,2^{k/2}\bigr)\le 9\,\alpha^{3/5}\beta^{2/5}\ll a^{3/10}G(a)^{1/5},
\]
with no factor $\log a$. The remaining terms contribute
$(\log_2a+1)(19+\gamma)\ll a^{1/100}\cdot a^{1/4}G(a)$. This is
$\ll a^{3/10}G(a)^{1/5}$, because $G(a)^{4/5}\ll a^{1/50}$.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{The Erd\H{o}s--Hooley function}
Let $\Delta_{\mathrm H}(n)=\max_{u\in\mathbb R}\#\{d\mid n:\ e^u<d\le e^{u+1}\}$. Taking
$e^{u+1}=2A$, and using $e>2$, gives $\tau_A(n)\le\Delta_{\mathrm H}(n)$. We use two published
theorems.

\begin{enumerate}
\item[(NT)] Nair and Tenenbaum~\cite{NT} prove a general upper bound for short sums of
functions $F$ satisfying $F(mn)\le\min(A^{\Omega(m)},Bm^\varepsilon)F(n)$ for $(m,n)=1$. They
note that $\Delta_{\mathrm H}$ is such a function. In their introduction they record the
consequence, for $Q(X)=X$ and $t=1$, that for $x$ large and $x^{\varepsilon}\le y\le x$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:NT}
\sum_{x<n\le x+y}\Delta_{\mathrm H}(n)\ll_\varepsilon\frac{y}{\log x}\sum_{n\le x}\frac{\Delta_{\mathrm H}(n)}{n}.
\end{equation}
We use it with $\varepsilon=1/4$.
\item[(BT)] de la Bret\`eche and Tenenbaum~\cite[Theorem~1.1]{BT} prove that for large $x$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BT}
\sum_{n\le x}\Delta_{\mathrm H}(n)\ll x\,(\log\log x)^{5/2}.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:partial}
Assume~\eqref{eq:BT}, and let $\ell(x)=\max(1,\log\log x)$. Then
$\sum_{n\le x}\Delta_{\mathrm H}(n)/n\ll\log x\cdot\ell(x)^{5/2}$ for large $x$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Split $n$ into dyadic blocks $2^j\le n<2^{j+1}\le 2x$. The terms with $n$ below the threshold
of~\eqref{eq:BT} contribute $O(1)$, since $\Delta_{\mathrm H}(n)\le n$. Each remaining block
contributes at most $2^{-j}\sum_{n\le 2^{j+1}}\Delta_{\mathrm H}(n)\ll\ell(2x)^{5/2}$, and
$\ell(2x)\le2\ell(x)$. There are at most $\log_2x+1\le 3\log x$ blocks.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:loglog}]
By~\eqref{eq:NT} and Lemma~\ref{lem:partial}, $\sum_{x<n\le x+y}\tau_A(n)\le
\sum_{x<n\le x+y}\Delta_{\mathrm H}(n)\ll y\,\ell(x)^{5/2}$ for $x^{1/4}\le y\le x$. So
$\mathrm{SD}(G)$ holds with $G=\ell^{5/2}$, which satisfies $G\ge1$ and $G(x)\ll x^{1/40}$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:generic} gives $P(a,b)\ll a^{3/10}\ell(a)^{1/2}\ll a^{3/10}\sqrt{\log\log3a}$.
\end{proof}

\section{Rises in the quotient increments}\label{sec:rises}

Throughout this section $k_i=q_{i+1}-q_i\ge1$, $d_i=b_i-b_{i+1}\ge1$, and a \emph{rise} is an
index with $k_i<k_{i+1}$.

\begin{lemma}[Six quotients in arithmetic progression]\label{lem:ap}
Let $q,h\ge1$ and $x_0>x_1>\dots>x_6\ge0$ satisfy $x_j(q+jh)+x_{j+1}=a$ for $0\le j\le5$. Then
$q^4\le 324\,h^3a$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $f_j=x_j-x_{j+1}$, and let $E=(f_1-f_2)h$ and $F=(f_2-f_3)h$ be the curvatures of the blocks
starting at $x_1$ and $x_2$ (all their increments equal $h$). With $U=f_0-2f_1+f_2$, the
division equations $e_j$ give the exact identity
\[
qhU=(3h-1)E+F,\qquad\text{namely}\qquad qhU-(3h-1)E-F=h(e_0-3e_1+3e_2-e_3).
\]
This is the case $u=v=w=z=h$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:six}, where $L=2h^3U$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:curv}, $E,F\ge1$, so the integer $U$ is at least $1$.

Now bound $E$ and $F$. Subtracting consecutive division equations gives
$q_jf_j\le hx_{j+1}$, where $q_j=q+jh$. Since $x_{j+1}q_{j+1}\le a$, this yields
$q^2f_j\le ha$ for $1\le j\le4$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:size},
$Ex_3\le(h+1)(f_1+f_2+f_3)^2\le 9h^2(h+1)a^2/q^4$. Also $a<x_3(q+3h+1)$, so
$Eq^4\le 9h^2(h+1)(q+3h+1)a$, and likewise $Fq^4\le 9h^2(h+1)(q+4h+1)a$. Hence
\[
q^5h\le q^4\bigl((3h-1)E+F\bigr)\le 27h^3(h+1)(q+4h+1)a .
\]
If $q\ge h$ this gives $q^4\le 324h^3a$. If $q<h$ then $q^4<h^4\le h^3a$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The source of this section proved a version of Lemma~\ref{lem:ap} for seven quotients by an
analytic argument: it represents a periodic continuation of the orbit by integrals of
positive functions, and compares finite differences. The identity above gives an algebraic
proof with one fewer quotient.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:norise}
Whenever $b_{i+2}>0$, $d_{i+1}k_i<d_ik_{i+1}$. In particular, if $k_{i+1}\le k_i$ then
$d_{i+1}<d_i$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
This is $D=d_ik_{i+1}-d_{i+1}k_i\ge1$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:curv}). If $k_{i+1}\le k_i$ and
$d_{i+1}\ge d_i$, then $d_ik_{i+1}\le d_{i+1}k_i$, which is a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:mult}
Let $c_j\ge1$ be integers, $j\in J$, such that no value occurs more than $M$ times. Then
$|J|^2\le 2M\sum_{j\in J}c_j$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Induct on $|J|$. Let $c_{j_0}$ be the largest value. The values lie in $[1,c_{j_0}]$, so
$|J|\le Mc_{j_0}$. Removing $j_0$ and using $|J|^2=(|J|-1)^2+2|J|-1$ completes the induction.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:rises}
Let $x_0,\dots,x_{T-1}$ be a window run in $[A,2A)$ with $T\ge2$, and let $\rho$ be the number
of rises among its $T-1$ increments. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $(T-1)^2\le 2(\rho+1)(A-1)$;
\item $T-1\le\lfloor a/A\rfloor$;
\item if $6A\le a$, then $T-1=\ell+s$ with $\ell^2\le 8(\rho+1)\lfloor a/A\rfloor$ and
$s^3\le 26244\,A$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Split the increments into \emph{runs}: maximal stretches containing no rise. There are at most
$\rho+1$ runs. Map each index to the end of its run.

(1) By Lemma~\ref{lem:norise}, the drops strictly decrease along a run, so each value of $d_j$
occurs at most once per run, hence at most $\rho+1$ times. Lemma~\ref{lem:mult} and
$\sum_jd_j=x_0-x_{T-1}\le A-1$ give the claim.

(2) The increments are $\ge1$, and they sum to $q_{T-1}-q_0\le\lfloor a/A\rfloor$.

(3) Call an increment $h$ \emph{low} if $26244h^3A^4<a^3$. Five consecutive equal increments
$h$ inside the run give six quotients in arithmetic progression. Lemma~\ref{lem:ap} then
gives $q^4\le324h^3a$. On the other hand, $a<3Aq$, which follows from $a=qx_0+x_1$,
$x_1<x_0<2A$ and $6A\le a$. Together these give $a^3\le26244h^3A^4$, so $h$ is not low.
Within a run the increments are nonincreasing, so equal values are consecutive. Hence each
low value occurs at most $4$ times per run, and at most $4(\rho+1)$ times in all.
Lemma~\ref{lem:mult} bounds the number $\ell$ of low increments by
$\ell^2\le8(\rho+1)\sum_jk_j\le8(\rho+1)\lfloor a/A\rfloor$. Let $s$ be the number of high
increments, with smallest value $h_*$. Then $sh_*\le\lfloor a/A\rfloor$ and
$a^3\le26244h_*^3A^4$, so $s^3a^3\le26244A^4(a/A)^3\le26244Aa^3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:rises}]
The rises inside a window are rises of the orbit, so $\rho+1\le R$. Combining the three parts
of Proposition~\ref{prop:rises}, as in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:generic}, gives for the
window $[2^k,2^{k+1})$
\[
T_k\le 1+\sqrt R\,\min\bigl(\sqrt{8a}\,2^{-k/2},\ \sqrt2\,2^{k/2}\bigr)
+\min\bigl(a\,2^{-k},\ 30\cdot2^{k/3}\bigr).
\]
The geometric-sum lemma, in its general form
$\sum_k\min(\alpha2^{-sk},\beta2^{tk})\ll_{s,t}\alpha^{t/(s+t)}\beta^{s/(s+t)}$, gives
$\sum_k\min(\sqrt{8a}\,2^{-k/2},\sqrt2\,2^{k/2})\ll(\sqrt{8a}\sqrt2)^{1/2}=2a^{1/4}$. It also gives
$\sum_k\min(a2^{-k},30\cdot2^{k/3})\ll a^{1/4}$. Since $\log_2a+1\ll a^{1/4}$, summing over $k$
gives the theorem.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:rises}, an orbit with $P(a,b)\asymp a^{3/10}$ must have $R\gg a^{1/10}$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:rises}(3) says more: inside the critical window $A\asymp a^{3/5}$, a
run of $T\asymp a^{3/10}$ remainders must contain $\gg a^{1/5}$ rises.
\end{remark}

\section{The Hankel hierarchy}\label{sec:hankel}

Let $b_0>b_1>\dots>b_{N-1}>0$ be the positive part of an orbit (indexed from $0$, so
$N=P(a,b)$), with quotients $q_i$. Put $p_i(j)=q_{i+j}-q_i$ for $j<N-i$. Define weights
$w_i(j)$ backwards by $w_{N-1}=\delta_0$ and
\[
w_i(0)=1-\sum_{j}\frac{w_{i+1}(j)}{p_i(j+1)},\qquad w_i(j+1)=\frac{w_{i+1}(j)}{p_i(j+1)} .
\]
Let $\mu_i$ be the measure with mass $w_i(j)$ at $p_i(j)$, and let
$\nu_i=\mu_i/(q_i+x)$. Write $h_i(s)=\int x^s\,d\nu_i=\sum_jw_i(j)p_i(j)^s/q_{i+j}$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:moments}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The weights are positive and sum to $1$.
\item $h_i(0)=b_i/a$, that is, $b_i/a=\mathbb E\bigl[1/(q_i+X_i)\bigr]$ for $X_i\sim\mu_i$.
\item $h_i(s+1)=\sum_{t\le s}\binom st k_i^{s-t}h_{i+1}(t)$ for $i+1<N$.
\item $h_i(s)=(-q_i)^sb_i/a+z$ with $z\in\mathbb Z$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(1) Since $p_i(j+1)\ge1$, the sum defining $w_i(0)$ is at most $1$. It is strictly less: if
$N-i-1\ge2$, the term $j=1$ has $p_i(2)\ge2$; if $i=N-2$, the last gap satisfies
$q_{N-1}\ge q_{N-2}+2$. (2) Since $p_i(j+1)=q_{i+j+1}-q_i$, the definitions give
$q_ih_i(0)+h_{i+1}(0)=1$, which is the normalised division equation; and
$h_{N-1}(0)=1/q_{N-1}=b_{N-1}/a$. (3) Use $p_i(j+1)=k_i+p_{i+1}(j)$ and the binomial theorem.
(4) Induct backwards on $i$ using (3), $b_{i+1}/a=1-q_ib_i/a$ and $k_i-q_{i+1}=-q_i$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:hankel}
For $1\le r\le N-i$, the number $I_r(i)=a\det\bigl(h_i(j+l)\bigr)_{0\le j,l<r}$ is a positive
integer. Moreover $I_1(i)=b_i$, $I_2(i)=d_i$ (if $i+1<N$), $I_3(i)=D_i$ (if $i+3<N$), and
$I_4(i)=L_i$ (if $i+5<N$), where $D_i$ and $L_i$ are the curvature and the six-step integer
of the blocks starting at $b_i$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Integrality.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:moments}(4),
$h_i(s+1)+q_ih_i(s)\in\mathbb Z$. Multiply the Hankel matrix on the left by the unit lower
triangular matrix that adds $q_i$ times row $j-1$ to row $j$. This makes rows
$1,\dots,r-1$ integral and leaves the determinant unchanged. Expand along row $0$, whose entries
are $(-q_i)^lb_i/a+(\text{integer})$; then $a\det$ is an integer.

\emph{Positivity.} The Hankel matrix is the Gram matrix of $1,x,\dots,x^{r-1}$ in $L^2(\nu_i)$.
Its quadratic form is $\sum_jw_i(j)q_{i+j}^{-1}\bigl(\sum_lc_lp_i(j)^l\bigr)^2$. If this
vanishes, the polynomial $\sum_lc_lx^l$ vanishes at the $N-i\ge r$ distinct points $p_i(j)$, so
it is zero, by the invertibility of the Vandermonde matrix. So the matrix is positive definite.

\emph{The first four.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:moments}(2),(3), the entries $h_i(s)$, $s\le2r-2$, are
explicit polynomials in $\beta_j=b_{i+j}/a$ and $k_i,\dots,k_{i+2r-3}$. The claimed values are
linear in the $b_{i+j}$. So each identity holds modulo the relations
$q_{i+j}\beta_j+\beta_{j+1}=1$, with explicit certificates of at most $156$ terms per
coefficient.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
So the positivity of $D$ and $L$, proved earlier by explicit identities, is an instance of the
positivity of Gram determinants. The integrality of $L$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:six}(1)) is an instance
of the integrality of $I_4$. A priori $I_r(i)$ involves the moments $h_i(s)$ for $s\le2r-2$,
and hence $b_i,\dots,b_{i+2r-2}$. In fact $I_3(i)$ depends only on $b_i,b_{i+1},b_{i+2}$ and
$q_i,q_{i+1},q_{i+2}$. Similarly $I_4(i)$ depends only on $b_i,\dots,b_{i+3}$ and
$q_i,\dots,q_{i+4}$: the later remainders cancel.
\end{remark}

\section{The Lean formalisation}\label{sec:lean}

The repository accompanying this paper contains a Lean~4 project (toolchain
\lean{v4.35.0}, Mathlib \lean{v4.35.0}) that proves Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. The final
statement is
\begin{quote}\ttfamily\small
theorem P\_le : $\forall$ $\varepsilon$ > (0 : $\mathbb R$), $\exists$ C > 0, $\forall$ a b : $\mathbb N$, 0 < b $\to$ b < a $\to$\\
\hspace*{2em}(P a b : $\mathbb R$) $\le$ C * (a : $\mathbb R$) \^{} ((3 : $\mathbb R$) / 10 + $\varepsilon$)
\end{quote}
where \lean{P a b} is defined by well-founded recursion as the number of positive terms
($P(a,0)=0$, $P(a,b)=P(a,a\bmod b)+1$ for $b>0$), and the file checks $P(35,22)=7$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:loglog} is formalised as
\begin{quote}\ttfamily\small
theorem P\_le\_loglog (hNT : NairTenenbaumDelta)\\
\hspace*{2em}(hBT : BretecheTenenbaumMean) :\\

Some details of the formalisation:
\begin{itemize}
\item The algebraic identities (Lemmas~\ref{lem:curv}, \ref{lem:fiber-eq}
and~\eqref{eq:resultant}) are proved over $\mathbb Z$ by the tactic
\lean{linear\_combination}, using the explicit coefficients above. They are then transferred to $\mathbb N$ by writing
$z=w+f$, $y=z+e$, $x=y+d$, which avoids truncated subtraction.
\item The curvature is the natural number $\lean{bD}\,a\,x=(x-y)v-(y-z)u$. Lean first proves
$eu<dv$, so this truncated subtraction is the true value.
\item The counting arguments use \lean{Finset.card\_le\_mul\_card\_image\_of\_maps\_to}: the
number of good indices is at most the size of the parameter box times the largest fiber.
\item The divisor bound $\tau(n)^k\le C_kn$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:div}) is the file
\lean{DivisorPowerBound.lean} from the \lean{openai/math} repository~\cite{OAIMath}
(Apache-2.0), vendored unchanged except for its import lines.
\item Lemma~\ref{lem:six}(2) is checked by \lean{linear\_combination} with the coefficients
given in its proof, which were found by polynomial division. Corollary~\ref{cor:six} proves
$L\ge1$ directly from the identity, since $L$ is an integer by definition.
\item The identities $I_2=d$, $I_3=D$, $I_4=L$ are checked by \lean{linear\_combination}, with
certificates generated by polynomial division. The moment entries $h_i(s)$ are computed in Lean
from the recursion of Lemma~\ref{lem:moments}(3).
\item The project has about 4100 lines of Lean. \lean{lake build} rebuilds it in under a
minute once Mathlib is built.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limits of the method}\label{sec:limits}

Proposition~\ref{prop:six} removes the divisor loss from the local bound, but the exponent is
still $3/10$. The local bound $T\ll a^{1/2}A^{-1/3}$ and the drop bound $T\ll A^{1/2}$ (up to
the divisor factor) both allow $T\asymp a^{3/10}$ at $A\asymp a^{3/5}$. Even an ideal
short-interval divisor estimate with $G$ constant leaves this balance unchanged. So sharpening
constants or improving the divisor counts cannot give a logarithmic bound. Further progress
needs to restrict \emph{which} configurations of the parameters $(u,v,w,z,D,E,F)$ can occur
together along a single orbit. Theorem~\ref{thm:rises} is one restriction of this kind: a long
orbit must have many rises in its quotient increments. Theorem~\ref{thm:hankel} supplies the
natural candidates for further ones, the higher invariants $I_r$.

There is no obvious monotonicity to exploit. The curvatures $D_i$ of successive blocks need
not decrease. For $a=13242$ the orbit of $255$ begins
\[
255,\ 237,\ 207,\ 201,\ 177,\ 144,\ 138,\ 132,\ \dots
\]
with quotients $51,55,63,65,74,91,95,100,\dots$. The curvatures of the first five blocks are
$24,12,6,111,30$, all for blocks that lie within a factor of two.
